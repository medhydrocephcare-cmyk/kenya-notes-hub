## 1. What is human resources management?

**Human resources management (HRM)** is the function responsible for managing an organisation's workforce effectively — attracting, developing, motivating and retaining the people needed to achieve organisational objectives, while ensuring fair, legally compliant treatment of employees throughout their employment.

---

## 2. Human resource planning

**HR planning** forecasts an organisation's future staffing needs (numbers, skills) and develops plans to meet them — through recruitment, training, or restructuring — ensuring the organisation has the right people, with the right skills, at the right time, rather than reacting to staffing gaps only after they become urgent problems.

---

## 3. Recruitment and selection

**Recruitment** is the process of attracting a pool of suitable candidates for a vacancy (e.g. through advertising, referrals, recruitment agencies). **Selection** is the process of choosing the most suitable candidate from that pool, typically using tools such as application review, interviews, and (for some roles) tests or assessment centres. A well-run selection process is structured and consistent across candidates, reducing the risk of bias and improving the likelihood of a genuinely suitable hire.

---

## 4. Induction and orientation

**Induction (orientation)** introduces a new employee to the organisation — its culture, policies, their specific role and team, and practical matters (systems access, workplace procedures). Effective induction reduces the time a new employee takes to become fully productive, and reduces early staff turnover caused by unclear expectations or a poor first impression of the organisation.

---

## 5. Training and development

**Training** builds specific skills/knowledge needed for an employee's current role; **development** builds broader capability for future roles/responsibilities. Common training methods: on-the-job training (learning while performing actual work, often with supervision/mentoring) and off-the-job training (structured courses/workshops away from the immediate work environment). Effective training should be tied to a genuine, identified skills gap, not delivered generically without reference to actual organisational needs.

---

## 6. Performance management and appraisal

**Performance management** is the ongoing process of setting clear expectations, monitoring performance, and providing feedback to help employees meet organisational objectives. A **performance appraisal** is a periodic, structured review of an employee's performance against agreed objectives, typically informing decisions on development needs, promotion, or (where necessary) performance improvement measures. Effective appraisal requires objective, specific criteria known in advance — vague or inconsistently applied criteria undermine both fairness and the appraisal's usefulness for genuine development planning.

---

## 7. Employee relations and discipline, basics

**Employee relations** covers the overall relationship between an organisation and its employees, including how grievances and disciplinary matters are handled. A fair **disciplinary process** typically requires: informing the employee clearly of the alleged issue, giving them a genuine opportunity to respond, and applying a proportionate, consistent outcome — natural justice principles that protect both the organisation (from unfair-dismissal claims) and the employee (from arbitrary treatment).

---

## 8. Human resources and the company secretarial function

A company secretary often has direct HR-adjacent responsibilities: ensuring board-level HR policies (e.g. executive remuneration, succession planning) are properly documented and approved through correct governance procedure, and supporting the remuneration committee (where one exists) in setting and disclosing senior executive pay. Understanding HR fundamentals helps a company secretary engage meaningfully with these governance-level HR matters, even without direct operational HR responsibility for the wider workforce.

---

## 9. Full worked question — evaluating a recruitment process weakness

**Scenario.** A company fills a management vacancy by directly appointing a manager's personal acquaintance, without advertising the role or interviewing any other candidates.

**Required:** (a) Identify the weakness in this recruitment approach. (b) Explain the risk this creates. (c) Recommend an improvement.

**Solution:**

(a) The weakness is a **lack of a structured, open recruitment and selection process** — the role was filled through a personal connection rather than a fair competitive process open to suitably qualified candidates.

(b) This creates a risk of hiring a less suitable candidate than might otherwise have been available, exposes the organisation to perceptions (or actual instances) of favouritism/nepotism, and can undermine staff morale and trust in the fairness of the organisation's processes generally.

(c) **Recommendation:** advertise the vacancy appropriately, define clear selection criteria in advance, and assess all candidates (including any personal acquaintances of existing staff) through the same structured, documented process to ensure the most suitable candidate is genuinely selected and the process is defensible if later questioned.

## 12. Exam technique and revision checklist

- Always distinguish related HR term pairs precisely: recruitment vs selection, training vs development, on-the-job vs off-the-job — these are frequently and specifically tested.
- Scenario questions (recruitment/discipline weaknesses): identify the specific weakness, then the risk, then a specific recommendation — a three-part structure examiners commonly expect.
- Appraisal/discipline questions: always mention fairness and consistency explicitly — a recurring theme across HR topics.
- CS-specific angle: always connect HR topics back to the company secretary's governance-support role (board HR policy documentation, remuneration committee support) when a question asks about the CS's involvement specifically.
- Don't answer HR questions purely generically — tie answers to the specific organisational risk or governance angle the question raises.

**Quick revision — one line per topic:**
1. HRM: managing workforce to achieve objectives, fairly and compliantly.
2. HR planning: forecasting future staffing needs and planning to meet them.
3. Recruitment (attracting candidates) vs selection (choosing the best one).
4. Induction: introduces new employee to org/role, reduces time-to-productivity and early turnover.
5. Training (current-role skills) vs development (future-role capability).
6. Performance appraisal: structured periodic review against agreed objectives.
7. Fair discipline: inform of issue, allow response, proportionate/consistent outcome.
8. Company secretary's HR role: governance-level (remuneration, succession), not operational HR.
