## 1. The communication process and barriers to it

**Communication** is the process of transmitting a message from sender to receiver so it is understood as intended: sender encodes → transmits via a channel → receiver decodes → feedback confirms understanding. Common barriers: physical (noise), language (jargon), perceptual/psychological (differing assumptions), and organisational (unclear reporting lines).

---

## 2. Business letters, memoranda and reports

A **business letter** is formal, typically for external correspondence, with a conventional structure (addresses, salutation, body, complimentary close). A **memorandum** is shorter, typically internal, using a simple To/From/Date/Subject header. A **report** presents information/analysis/recommendations, typically structured as: title, terms of reference, procedure, findings, conclusions/recommendations.

---

## 3. Meetings and minute-taking

Key meeting roles: the **chairperson** (leads the meeting, ensures order) and the **secretary** (prepares the agenda, records minutes). A **quorum** is the minimum attendance required for valid business. **Minutes** are the official record of what was discussed and decided, typically including attendance, matters arising, resolutions passed, and actions assigned with a responsible person and deadline.

---

## 4. What is records management?

**Records management** is the systematic control of an organisation's records — their creation, maintenance, use and disposal — ensuring records are accurate, accessible when needed, and retained (or destroyed) in line with legal and organisational requirements. Good records management supports accountability, decision-making, and compliance, while poor records management creates real legal and operational risk (e.g. inability to produce required documents during a dispute or audit).

---

## 5. Filing systems and classification methods

Common classification methods: **alphabetical** (by name), **numerical** (by assigned number, often with a separate alphabetical index), **subject** (grouped by topic/matter), and **chronological** (by date). Each has trade-offs: alphabetical filing is intuitive but can suffer from inconsistent naming; numerical filing supports confidentiality (files aren't identifiable by name alone) but requires an index; subject filing groups related material logically but requires clear, consistently applied categories.

---

## 6. The records life cycle

Records typically pass through: **creation** (a record is generated), **active use** (frequently accessed for current business), **semi-active storage** (accessed occasionally, often moved to lower-cost storage), and **disposal** (destruction or permanent archival, per a retention schedule). Managing records deliberately through this life cycle — rather than treating all records as equally "live" indefinitely — keeps active filing systems efficient and storage costs manageable.

---

## 7. Records retention and disposal

A **retention schedule** specifies how long different record types must be kept before disposal, based on legal, regulatory, and operational requirements (e.g. tax records typically require longer retention than routine internal correspondence). Records should never be destroyed without reference to an approved retention schedule, since premature disposal can create serious legal exposure if a record is later required for a dispute, audit, or regulatory inquiry.

---

## 8. Electronic records management, basics

Electronic records management involves similar principles to physical records — consistent classification, access control, and retention/disposal policy — but adds specific considerations: version control (ensuring the current version is clearly identified), backup and disaster recovery, and information security (protecting confidential records from unauthorised access). A well-designed electronic system should make retrieval faster and more reliable than an equivalent physical system, provided classification and naming conventions are applied consistently.

## 9. Full worked question — drafting minutes of a meeting

**Scenario.** A department held a meeting to decide on relocating to a new office floor. Present: 5 members (quorum = 4). It was resolved to proceed with relocation by 30 September, with the facilities officer responsible for coordination.

**Required:** Draft a short extract of minutes reflecting this decision.

**Solution:**

**MINUTES EXTRACT**

**Present:** 5 members (quorum of 4 met)

**Matter discussed:** Proposed relocation to the new office floor.

**Resolution:** It was resolved that the department shall relocate to the new office floor by **30 September**. The **Facilities Officer** was assigned responsibility for coordinating the relocation and reporting progress at the next meeting.

## 12. Exam technique and revision checklist

- Meeting-related questions: know the specific roles and terms (chairperson, secretary, quorum, minutes) precisely.
- Records-management "outline" questions: name specific classification methods or life-cycle stages, not a vague general description.
- Retention schedule questions: always mention both the legal/compliance angle and the risk of premature disposal.
- Electronic records questions: distinguish clearly what's *additional* to physical records (version control, backup, security), not just repeat physical-records principles.
- Scenario/minutes questions: always include quorum status, the specific resolution, and the responsible person/deadline where given.
