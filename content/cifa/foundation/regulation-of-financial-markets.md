## 1. Why financial markets are regulated

Financial markets are regulated to protect investors, maintain fair and orderly markets, reduce systemic risk, and build public confidence in the financial system. Without regulation, information asymmetries (some participants knowing more than others), conflicts of interest, and outright fraud could undermine trust to the point that investors withdraw from markets entirely, harming the wider economy's ability to raise and allocate capital efficiently.

---

## 2. Key financial regulators in Kenya

- **Capital Markets Authority (CMA)** — regulates capital markets (securities, collective investment schemes, listed companies).
- **Central Bank of Kenya (CBK)** — regulates banks and oversees monetary policy.
- **Insurance Regulatory Authority (IRA)** — regulates insurance companies and intermediaries.
- **Retirement Benefits Authority (RBA)** — regulates pension schemes.
- **Sacco Societies Regulatory Authority (SASRA)** — regulates deposit-taking SACCOs.

Each regulator has a distinct mandate, though their regulated sectors increasingly interact (e.g. pension funds and insurers investing in capital-markets instruments regulated by the CMA).

---

## 3. The role of the Capital Markets Authority (CMA)

The **CMA** is the primary regulator of Kenya's capital markets, responsible for: licensing and supervising market intermediaries (stockbrokers, investment banks, fund managers), approving public offers of securities, enforcing disclosure and governance requirements for listed companies, and investigating/enforcing action against market abuse (insider trading, market manipulation). The CMA's overarching objectives are investor protection, market integrity, and the orderly development of Kenya's capital markets.

---

## 4. The Nairobi Securities Exchange (NSE), basics

The **Nairobi Securities Exchange (NSE)** is Kenya's licensed securities exchange, providing a regulated marketplace for trading listed equities, bonds and other securities. Companies seeking to list must meet the NSE's listing requirements (covering matters such as minimum capital, shareholder spread, and financial track record) and, once listed, are subject to ongoing disclosure and governance obligations designed to keep investors properly informed.

---

## 5. Licensing and market intermediaries

Market intermediaries — stockbrokers, investment banks, fund managers, investment advisers — must generally be licensed by the CMA before providing regulated services, subject to minimum capital, competence, and conduct requirements. Licensing exists to give investors assurance that anyone handling their investments or providing investment advice has met a baseline standard of competence and financial soundness, rather than being an entirely unregulated intermediary.

---

## 6. Market abuse — insider trading and market manipulation

**Insider trading** — trading a security while in possession of material non-public information about it, or improperly disclosing such information — is prohibited because it gives an unfair advantage over other market participants who lack access to the same information. **Market manipulation** — actions intended to artificially affect a security's price or trading volume (e.g. spreading false information, or coordinated trading intended to create a misleading impression of demand) — is similarly prohibited, since it undermines the fair, efficient functioning of the market that all participants depend on for reliable pricing.

---

## 7. Investor protection mechanisms

Common investor protection mechanisms include: mandatory disclosure requirements (ensuring investors have access to material information before investing), licensing/conduct requirements for intermediaries, market surveillance and enforcement against abuse, and, in some markets, investor compensation funds providing limited protection if a licensed intermediary fails. Collectively, these mechanisms aim to reduce information asymmetry and give investors reasonable confidence that markets are being properly policed.

---

## 8. Disclosure and continuing obligations for listed companies

Listed companies face **continuing disclosure obligations** — requirements to promptly disclose material information (e.g. significant financial results, major transactions, changes in directorship) that could reasonably affect the company's share price, so that all investors have fair, timely access to material information rather than only a privileged few. Regular reporting obligations (e.g. periodic financial statements) further support ongoing investor confidence by keeping the market continuously informed, rather than relying solely on the initial listing disclosure.

## 9. Full worked question — identifying a regulatory breach

**Scenario.** A company's finance director learns, ahead of the public announcement, that the company's annual profit will be significantly below market expectations. Before the announcement is made public, the finance director sells a large portion of their personal shareholding in the company.

**Required:** (a) Identify the regulatory issue in this scenario. (b) Explain why this conduct is prohibited.

**Solution:**

(a) This is a case of **insider trading** — the finance director traded (sold) shares while in possession of material non-public information (the significantly lower-than-expected profit figure) ahead of its public disclosure.

(b) This conduct is prohibited because it gives the finance director an unfair advantage over other market participants who did not have access to the same information — those other participants effectively bought or held the shares from/against someone who knew material negative information they did not, undermining the fairness and integrity of the market and investor confidence in it generally.

## 12. Exam technique and revision checklist

- Regulator questions: know each Kenyan regulator's specific mandate precisely (CMA/capital markets, CBK/banks, IRA/insurance, RBA/pensions, SASRA/SACCOs).
- Insider trading/market manipulation: always explain *why* the conduct is prohibited (unfair advantage / undermines market integrity), not just define the term.
- Investor protection questions: name specific mechanisms rather than a vague "the regulator protects investors."
- Disclosure questions: connect continuing disclosure obligations to fair, timely investor access to material information.
- Scenario questions: identify the specific regulatory issue by name first, then explain the underlying reasoning.
