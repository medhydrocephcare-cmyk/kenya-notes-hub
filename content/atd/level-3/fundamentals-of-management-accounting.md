## 1. Management accounting versus financial accounting

**Financial accounting** produces statements (profit or loss, financial position) mainly for external users (investors, tax authorities, regulators), following prescribed formats and standards, focused on the past. **Management accounting** produces information mainly for internal users (managers), with no fixed format requirement, focused on supporting planning, control and decision-making — often forward-looking (budgets, forecasts) as well as backward-looking (variance analysis of what already happened).

---

## 2. Classification of costs

- **By behaviour:** **Fixed costs** remain constant in total regardless of activity level within a relevant range (e.g. rent); **variable costs** change in direct proportion to activity level (e.g. direct materials); **semi-variable costs** contain both a fixed and a variable element (e.g. a utility bill with a standing charge plus a usage-based charge).
- **By element:** **Materials**, **labour**, and **expenses**.
- **By traceability:** **Direct costs** can be traced specifically and economically to a particular product/service/department; **indirect costs (overheads)** cannot be so traced and must be shared/apportioned across multiple cost objects.

---

## 3. Cost-volume-profit (CVP) analysis and break-even

CVP analysis examines how costs, sales volume and profit interact.

**Contribution per unit** = Selling price per unit − Variable cost per unit

**Break-even point (units)** = Total fixed costs ÷ Contribution per unit

**Break-even point (sales value)** = Break-even units × Selling price per unit

**Margin of safety** = Budgeted (or actual) sales − Break-even sales — the amount by which sales can fall before the business starts making a loss.

---

## 4. Marginal costing versus absorption costing

**Marginal costing** treats only variable production costs as part of the cost of a unit, with fixed production overheads treated as a period cost written off in full against the period's profit. **Absorption costing** treats both variable AND a fair share of fixed production overheads as part of the cost of a unit, so fixed overheads are "absorbed" into inventory values and only hit profit as units are sold, rather than the whole amount hitting profit in the period incurred.

A key practical consequence: when production exceeds sales in a period (inventory increases), absorption costing generally reports a higher profit than marginal costing, because some fixed overhead is carried forward in the closing inventory value rather than being expensed immediately.

---

## 5. Budgeting, basics

A **budget** is a formal, quantified plan for a future period, expressed in financial and/or physical terms, used to plan operations, coordinate activities, communicate targets, motivate performance, and provide a benchmark for control (comparing actual results against budget). Common budgets include the **sales budget**, **production budget**, **materials/labour/overhead budgets**, and the **cash budget** (forecasting cash inflows and outflows, and the resulting cash position, over the budget period).

---

## 6. Standard costing and variance analysis, introduction

A **standard cost** is a predetermined, carefully estimated cost for a unit of output, set under specified operating conditions — used as a benchmark against which actual costs are compared. **Variance analysis** compares actual results to the standard/budgeted figures, computing a **variance** (the difference) for each cost element, then investigating the underlying cause. A variance is described as **favourable** (F) if it improves profit compared to standard (e.g. actual cost lower than standard) or **adverse** (A) if it worsens profit compared to standard (e.g. actual cost higher than standard).

---

## 7. Material and labour variances

**Material price variance** = (Standard price − Actual price) × Actual quantity purchased

**Material usage variance** = (Standard quantity for actual output − Actual quantity used) × Standard price

**Labour rate variance** = (Standard rate − Actual rate) × Actual hours worked

**Labour efficiency variance** = (Standard hours for actual output − Actual hours worked) × Standard rate

In each case, a positive result (using the formula direction shown) indicates a favourable variance; a negative result indicates an adverse variance.

---

## 8. The purpose of variance analysis

Variance analysis supports **management by exception** — rather than reviewing every single cost item in detail, management can focus attention specifically on significant variances (favourable or adverse), investigating their causes and taking corrective action where an adverse variance reveals a genuine, controllable problem (e.g. inefficient material usage, or a change in supplier pricing worth renegotiating). It's worth noting a favourable variance isn't automatically "good news" without investigation either — for example, a favourable material price variance from buying unusually cheap, lower-quality material might later cause an adverse usage variance through increased wastage.

## 9. Full worked question — break-even and margin of safety

**Scenario.** A company produces a single product with a selling price of KSh 500 per unit and variable cost of KSh 300 per unit. Total fixed costs are KSh 800,000 per month. Budgeted sales for the month are 5,000 units.

**Required:** (a) Compute the contribution per unit. (b) Compute the break-even point in units and in sales value. (c) Compute the margin of safety in units and as a percentage of budgeted sales.

**Solution:**

(a) Contribution per unit = 500 − 300 = **KSh 200**

(b) Break-even units = 800,000 ÷ 200 = **4,000 units**. Break-even sales value = 4,000 × 500 = **KSh 2,000,000**.

(c) Margin of safety (units) = Budgeted sales − Break-even sales = 5,000 − 4,000 = **1,000 units**.

Margin of safety (%) = (1,000 ÷ 5,000) × 100 = **20%** — meaning budgeted sales could fall by up to 20% before the company starts making a loss.

## 11. Marking guide — model answers to the practice examination

**QUESTION ONE**

(a) *(8 marks)* — Financial accounting: external users, prescribed formats, historical focus. Management accounting: internal users (managers), no fixed format, focused on planning/control/decision-making, both forward and backward-looking.

(b) *(9 marks — 3 marks each)* — Fixed: constant in total regardless of activity (e.g. rent). Variable: changes in direct proportion to activity (e.g. direct materials). Semi-variable: contains both a fixed and variable element (e.g. a utility bill).

(c) *(3 marks)* — A direct cost can be traced specifically and economically to a particular cost object; an indirect cost (overhead) cannot and must be shared/apportioned.

**QUESTION TWO**

(a) *(3 marks)* — 800 − 480 = **KSh 320**.

(b) *(8 marks)* — Break-even units = 960,000 ÷ 320 = **3,000 units**. Break-even sales value = 3,000 × 800 = **KSh 2,400,000**.

(c) *(6 marks)* — Margin of safety (units) = 4,000 − 3,000 = **1,000 units**. As a percentage = (1,000 ÷ 4,000) × 100 = **25%**.

(d) *(3 marks)* — It shows how far budgeted sales can fall before the company starts making a loss — a measure of the "cushion" between planned sales and the break-even point.

**QUESTION THREE**

(a) *(10 marks)* — Marginal costing treats fixed production overheads as a period cost, written off in full against the period's profit; absorption costing treats a fair share of fixed production overheads as part of unit cost, "absorbed" into inventory value and expensed only as units are sold.

(b) *(10 marks)* — When production exceeds sales, some units remain in closing inventory; under absorption costing, part of the period's fixed overhead is carried forward within that closing inventory value rather than being expensed in the period, so reported profit is higher than under marginal costing, which expenses the whole fixed overhead regardless of production/sales volume.

**QUESTION FOUR**

(a) *(6 marks)* — (Standard price − Actual price) × Actual quantity = (25 − 24) × 4,300 = **KSh 4,300 Favourable** (actual price was lower than standard).

(b) *(8 marks)* — Standard quantity for actual output = 1,000 × 4 = 4,000 kg. (Standard quantity − Actual quantity) × Standard price = (4,000 − 4,300) × 25 = −300 × 25 = **KSh 7,500 Adverse** (more material used than standard allows).

(c) *(6 marks)* — Material price variance: **Favourable**. Material usage variance: **Adverse**.

**QUESTION FIVE**

(a) *(12 marks — 3 marks each)* — Planning operations; coordinating activities across departments; communicating targets; motivating performance (providing a control benchmark also acceptable).

(b) *(8 marks)* — Management by exception means focusing attention specifically on significant variances rather than reviewing every cost item in detail; a favourable variance still deserves investigation because it may result from a decision with a hidden downside elsewhere (e.g. cheaper, lower-quality material causing a later adverse usage variance through increased wastage).

## 12. Exam technique and revision checklist

- Always state the relevant formula explicitly (contribution, break-even, variance formulas) before substituting numbers — marks are awarded for correct method, not just the final figure.
- Break-even/margin of safety questions: compute contribution per unit first — almost every subsequent figure in this topic depends on it being correct.
- Marginal vs absorption costing: the key distinguishing point is the treatment of *fixed production overhead* specifically — always anchor your answer to that single distinction.
- Variance questions: always state clearly whether each variance is favourable or adverse, and be consistent with the sign convention you've used in your calculation.
- Budgeting/variance "explain the purpose" questions: name specific purposes (planning, coordination, communication, motivation, control) rather than a vague "it helps the business run better."
