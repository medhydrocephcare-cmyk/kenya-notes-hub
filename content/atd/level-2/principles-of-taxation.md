## 1. What is a tax, and why do governments levy them?

A **tax** is a compulsory financial charge imposed by a government on individuals or entities, without a direct, specific benefit given in return, used to fund public services and government operations (infrastructure, security, healthcare, education). Taxes are also used as policy tools — for example, to discourage consumption of certain goods, or to redistribute income through progressive tax structures.

---

## 2. Classification of taxes

- **Direct taxes** — levied directly on income or wealth of the person/entity bearing the tax (e.g. income tax); the burden cannot easily be shifted to someone else.
- **Indirect taxes** — levied on goods and services, ultimately borne by the final consumer even though collected by an intermediary (e.g. VAT, excise duty); the burden can be shifted along the supply chain.
- **Progressive tax** — the rate of tax increases as income increases (higher earners pay a higher percentage).
- **Proportional (flat) tax** — the same percentage rate applies regardless of income level.
- **Regressive tax** — takes a larger percentage of income from lower-income earners than higher-income earners (often a criticism levelled at certain indirect taxes on necessities).

---

## 3. Kenya's tax administration — KRA

The **Kenya Revenue Authority (KRA)** is the government body responsible for assessing, collecting and accounting for tax revenue in Kenya, and for administering tax laws generally. KRA's functions include registering taxpayers (issuing a Personal Identification Number, PIN), processing tax returns, conducting assessments and audits, and enforcing compliance, including penalties for non-compliance.

---

## 4. Income tax, basics — chargeable income

**Income tax** is charged on income accruing in or derived from Kenya (with additional rules for residents' worldwide income in certain cases). Chargeable income for an individual typically includes: employment income, business income, rental income, and investment income (subject to specific rules for each category — some income types are taxed under distinct, separate regimes rather than combined into one single computation).

Chargeable (taxable) income is generally computed as: Gross income − Allowable deductions/reliefs = Chargeable income, to which the applicable tax rate(s) are then applied.

---

## 5. Pay As You Earn (PAYE), the concept

**PAYE** is the system under which an employer deducts income tax from an employee's salary/wages at source, and remits it directly to KRA on the employee's behalf, spreading the employee's tax liability evenly across their pay periods rather than requiring one large payment at year-end. PAYE typically operates on graduated (progressive) tax bands — different portions of income are taxed at increasing rates as income rises — after allowing for permitted reliefs (e.g. a personal relief) that reduce the final tax payable.

**Illustrative graduated bands used for teaching purposes in this document** (not to be relied on for an actual computation — always confirm current bands with KRA):

| Monthly taxable pay (KSh) | Illustrative rate |
|---|---|
| First 24,000 | 10% |
| Next 8,333 (24,001–32,333) | 25% |
| Above 32,333 | 30% |

---

## 6. Value Added Tax (VAT), basics

**VAT** is an indirect tax charged on the supply of taxable goods and services at each stage of production and distribution, ultimately borne by the final consumer. A VAT-registered business charges **output VAT** on its sales and can generally reclaim **input VAT** paid on its own taxable purchases — remitting to KRA only the net difference (output VAT − input VAT) for the period. Some supplies are **zero-rated** (taxed at 0%, with input VAT still reclaimable) while others are **exempt** (no VAT charged, and input VAT on related purchases generally cannot be reclaimed) — the distinction matters for how a business accounts for VAT on its related costs.

---

## 7. Tax returns, assessments and compliance

Taxpayers are generally required to file periodic **tax returns** (e.g. annual income tax returns, monthly VAT/PAYE returns) declaring their income/transactions and computing the tax due. KRA may conduct an **assessment** — reviewing a taxpayer's affairs and determining the tax actually payable, which may differ from what the taxpayer originally declared if errors or omissions are found. Timely, accurate filing and payment is important both to avoid penalties and to maintain good compliance standing (relevant, for instance, to qualifying for certain business licenses or tenders that require tax compliance certificates).

---

## 8. Tax offences and penalties, basics

Common tax offences include: late filing of returns, late payment of tax due, under-declaration of income, and failure to register for tax where required. Consequences typically include financial **penalties** (often calculated as a percentage of the tax involved, or a fixed amount) and **interest** on unpaid tax accruing over the period of default — with more serious or deliberate offences (e.g. tax evasion) potentially attracting more severe legal consequences beyond financial penalties alone.

## 9. Full worked question — illustrative PAYE-style computation

**Scenario.** An employee earns a gross monthly salary of KSh 50,000, with no other allowances or deductions besides tax. Using the illustrative bands in Section 5 above (for teaching purposes only).

**Required:** Compute the employee's tax liability before any personal relief, using the illustrative bands provided.

**Solution:**

- First 24,000 at 10% = 2,400
- Next 8,333 (24,001 to 32,333) at 25% = 2,083.25
- Remaining (50,000 − 32,333 = 17,667) at 30% = 5,300.10

**Total tax before relief = 2,400 + 2,083.25 + 5,300.10 = KSh 9,783.35** (illustrative figure only — actual liability would then be reduced by any personal relief and other permitted reliefs applicable under current law).

## 12. Exam technique and revision checklist

- Always state clearly that illustrative tax rates/bands used in study material must be verified against the current Finance Act/KRA guidance before real-world use — examiners expect awareness that rates change, not memorised outdated figures presented as permanently fixed.
- Graduated-band computations: work through each band in order, and only apply the remaining rate to the portion of income still uncovered by lower bands — a common error is applying the top rate to the entire income.
- Direct vs indirect tax: the key distinguishing test is whether the burden can be shifted to someone else (indirect) or not (direct).
- VAT questions: always define output VAT and input VAT clearly, and know the zero-rated vs exempt distinction precisely — a very commonly tested point.
- Compliance questions: pair each named offence with its typical consequence (penalty/interest) rather than listing offences alone.

**Quick revision — one line per topic:**
1. Tax = compulsory charge, no direct specific benefit in return, funds public services and policy goals.
2. Direct tax (on income/wealth, burden not shifted) vs indirect tax (on goods/services, burden shifted to consumer).
3. Progressive (rate rises with income) vs proportional (flat rate) vs regressive (falls harder on lower earners).
4. KRA: registers taxpayers, processes returns, conducts assessments, enforces compliance.
5. Chargeable income = Gross income − allowable deductions/reliefs.
6. PAYE: employer deducts tax at source and remits to KRA on graduated bands.
7. VAT: output VAT − input VAT = net VAT remitted; zero-rated (0%, input reclaimable) vs exempt (no VAT, input generally not reclaimable).
8. Tax offences: late filing/payment, under-declaration, failure to register — attract penalties and interest.
