## 1. Why "specialised" activities need their own rules

The general rules on gains and profits from business apply to most trading activity, but certain sectors and structures raise **timing, valuation, or entity-classification** issues that the general rules cannot handle cleanly. The Income Tax Act therefore provides *specific* rules for these cases so that the correct amount of tax is charged on the correct person at the correct time.

Recurring themes across "specialised" activities:
- **Who is the taxable person** — the entity, or its members?
- **Timing** — is income recognised on cash, accrual, or a special basis?
- **Special deductions** — sector-specific capital allowances or reliefs.
- **Special rates** — reduced corporate rates, tax holidays, or ring-fencing.
- **Anti-avoidance overlays** — group loss relief, transfer pricing, thin capitalisation.

## 2. Taxation of partnerships

For income-tax purposes a **partnership is not a taxable person**. It is a "look-through" vehicle: profits are computed at the partnership level and then **assessed on the individual partners**.

**Definition (English/Kenyan law):** partnership is *the relationship that subsists between persons carrying on a business in common with a view to profit*.

**Under Section 4(b) of the Act, the gain or profits of a partner from a partnership is:**

1. Remuneration payable to the partner by the partnership + interest on capital payable to the partner by the partnership − interest on capital payable *by* the partner to the partnership; **plus**
2. The partner's **share of the total income of the partnership**, calculated *after* deducting the total of any remuneration and interest on capital payable to any partner and *after* adding any interest on capital payable *by* any partner to the partnership.

Where the partnership makes a loss on part (2), the partner's gains or profits are the excess, if any, of part (1) over the partner's share of that loss.

**Existence of a partnership** is a question of fact. Indicators:
- A written partnership deed.
- Joint tenancy or tenancy in common of business assets.
- Sharing of gross receipts or profits.

None is conclusive on its own — the courts look at the substance of the arrangement (proprietorship attributes + profit motive).

**Common grey areas:**
- Joint one-off transactions (are the participants partners or just parties to a joint venture?).
- Parties described as partners in name but functioning as employees (or vice versa).

**Consequences of misclassification:**
- Employee treatment triggers PAYE.
- Partnership treatment triggers self-assessment on each partner.
- Company treatment triggers corporation tax + WHT on dividends.

## 3. Taxation of groups of companies

A **group of companies** arises where one company (the parent) controls one or more other companies (subsidiaries), typically through majority ownership. In Kenya, each company in a group is a **separate taxable person** — group relief in the UK sense is **not** available.

**Key features:**
- Each company files its own return and computes its own tax.
- **Inter-company dividends** paid by a resident subsidiary to a resident holding company that controls ≥12.5% (verify current threshold) of the voting power are generally **exempt from tax** in the recipient's hands and outside the WHT charge.
- **Losses** cannot be surrendered from one group member to another (no group loss relief).
- **Transfer pricing rules** (see the dedicated cross-border notes) apply to inter-company transactions.
- **Thin capitalisation** rules restrict interest deductions where a resident company is controlled by a non-resident and its debt-to-equity ratio exceeds the statutory limit.

**Reorganisations within a group** may qualify for specific reliefs (e.g. rollover for transfers of qualifying assets between group companies) — always verify the current statutory basis.

## 4. Taxation of farmers

Farming is treated as a business, but its cash-flow and asset profile are unusual. Special rules:

- **Livestock and produce on hand** at year-end are included in stock at cost or net realisable value, whichever is lower.
- **Farm works deductions** are available on qualifying capital expenditure on farm buildings, water works, fences, and land improvements — check the current schedule and rates.
- **Growing crops** may be valued at cost of production; standing timber is generally recognised on harvest.
- **Averaging** relief may be available to smooth exceptional years (verify current statutory position).
- Farmers may opt for cash-basis assessment in narrowly defined circumstances.

**Compensation and insurance recoveries** for destroyed livestock or crops are usually taxable in the year of receipt, subject to any reinvestment rollover the Act may permit.

## 5. Taxation of professionals in private practice

Professionals (lawyers, accountants, doctors, consultants) trading in own name or as a partnership are taxed on the **net income** of the practice:

- **Fee income** — recognised on invoice/accrual basis in most cases.
- **Work in progress** — unbilled time on live matters must generally be brought in at year-end.
- **Withholding tax** — many professional fee payments attract WHT at source; the WHT is a *credit* against the professional's final tax.
- **Allowable deductions** — practising certificate fees, indemnity insurance, CPD, staff costs, office rent, depreciation-equivalent (wear and tear) on equipment.
- **Not allowable** — provisions for future bad debts (only specific written-off debts qualify), private-use portions of motor and phone costs.

Where the professional practises through an incorporated company, corporation tax applies to the company and PAYE to any employment income drawn.

## 6. Taxation of leasing activities

For income tax, the treatment turns on whether a lease is an **operating lease** or a **finance lease**.

- **Operating lease** — the lessor retains ownership economically as well as legally. Rentals received are business income taxed at the corporate rate; lessor claims wear-and-tear on the asset.
- **Finance lease** — economically a sale on credit. The lessee typically capitalises the asset and claims wear-and-tear; the lessor's interest element is taxed as investment income while the principal repayment portion is not.

Careful classification matters: the wrong treatment double-counts deductions or misses them entirely.

## 7. Export Processing Zone (EPZ) enterprises

EPZ enterprises get a package of incentives designed to attract export-oriented investment:

- **10-year corporation tax holiday** — 0% for the first 10 years of operation.
- **Reduced corporation tax rate** of 25% (illustrative — verify current rate) for the next 10 years.
- **Exemption from withholding tax** on dividends and other payments to non-residents for the initial 10-year period.
- **Duty and VAT exemption** on imports of plant, machinery, and raw materials used in export production.
- **Exemption from stamp duty** on relevant legal instruments.

**Conditions:** the enterprise must be licensed by the EPZ Authority, must export a specified minimum percentage of output (typically ≥80%), and must operate within a designated EPZ. Sales into the Kenyan domestic market are treated as imports subject to normal duties and VAT.

Special Economic Zones (SEZs) offer a parallel but distinct incentive framework — do not conflate the two in an exam answer.

## 8. Taxation of financial institutions

Banks and other regulated financial institutions face sector-specific rules:

- **Interest income** — recognised on accrual, subject to CBK-prescribed classification (performing vs non-performing).
- **Loan loss provisions** — only **specific provisions** for identified impaired loans are deductible; general provisions are added back.
- **Excess bank levy** and other sector levies are non-deductible unless the Act specifies otherwise.
- **Digital service tax / significant economic presence** rules may apply to cross-border digital financial services (verify current statutory position).

## 9. Taxation of insurance businesses

Insurance is bifurcated for tax:

**Life assurance business** — profits computed under a specific formula that recognises investment income of the life fund and, in some structures, uses actuarial valuation. Historically, life assurance profits attracted special reduced rates; verify current position.

**General insurance business** — taxed broadly like ordinary trading, with adjustments for:
- Unearned premium reserves.
- Outstanding claims reserves (IBNR and reported-but-not-settled).
- Reinsurance recoveries.

Investment income of the general insurance fund is taxable at the corporate rate. Composite insurers must ring-fence the two funds.

## 10. Instalment tax for companies

Companies pay corporation tax in **four equal quarterly instalments** during the year of income:

- **Due dates:** 20th day of the 4th, 6th, 9th, and 12th months of the accounting period.
- **Basis:** the lower of (i) the current-year estimated tax, and (ii) 110% of the previous year's tax liability.
- **Balance:** any unpaid balance is due by the last day of the 4th month after year-end (with the return).
- **Exception:** agricultural companies pay in **two** instalments — 75% by the 9th month and 25% by the 12th month.
- **Penalties:** underpayment attracts interest at the prescribed statutory rate.

## 11. Compensating tax and dividend distributions

"**Compensating tax**" (historical Kenyan concept — check whether it still exists in the current Act) was a top-up charge levied on a company that distributed dividends out of untaxed profits (e.g. exempt income). Its purpose was to prevent shareholders from receiving fully imputed dividends where the underlying corporate profits had never been taxed.

Where it applies:
- Track a company's **Dividend Tax Account** (DTA) or equivalent memorandum record.
- On distribution, if dividends declared exceed the taxed-profits pool, compensating tax equalises the difference.
- Currently, resident-to-resident dividend flows and distributions from taxed profits are typically outside compensating tax.

## 12. Investment deductions and wear-and-tear allowances

*Rates below are illustrative — always cross-check the current Second Schedule to the Income Tax Act.*

**Investment deduction (ID)** — on qualifying capital expenditure on industrial buildings, machinery, and civil works in specified locations. Historical rates included 100% (standard) and 150% (satellite towns adjoining Nairobi, Mombasa, or Kisumu). The current Act may have replaced this with a consolidated capital allowances regime — verify.

**Wear and tear** — computed on the reducing-balance basis by asset class (illustrative classes):
- Class I (heavy self-propelling machinery): 37.5%
- Class II (computers, copiers, faxes): 30%
- Class III (light self-propelling motor vehicles): 25% (capped for restricted vehicles)
- Class IV (all other rigid plant, furniture, and fittings): 12.5%

**Mining allowance** — for capital expenditure on mining operations, deducted over the life of the mine on a prescribed schedule.

**Farm works** — accelerated deduction on qualifying farm capital expenditure.

## 13. Worked question — partnership assessment

**Scenario (original).** Amina and Baraka trade in partnership sharing profits and losses 3:2 after allowing:
- Salary to Amina: KSh 480,000
- Interest on capital: Amina 10% on KSh 1,000,000; Baraka 10% on KSh 800,000
- Interest charged on drawings: Amina KSh 20,000; Baraka KSh 15,000

For the year ended 31 December 2026 the partnership's adjusted trading profit (before partner appropriations) is **KSh 2,635,000**.

**Required:** Compute the total income assessable on each partner.

**Model answer**

*Step 1 — appropriate the profit*
```
Adjusted trading profit                              2,635,000
Less: Amina's salary                       (480,000)
Less: Interest on capital  Amina  100,000
                            Baraka  80,000  (180,000)
Add:  Interest on drawings Amina   20,000
                            Baraka  15,000    35,000
                                             ---------
Residual profit for sharing                          2,010,000

Amina 3/5 × 2,010,000 = 1,206,000
Baraka 2/5 × 2,010,000 =   804,000
```

*Step 2 — total income of each partner*
```
                                Amina       Baraka
Salary                          480,000           -
Interest on capital             100,000      80,000
Less: Interest on drawings      (20,000)    (15,000)
Share of residual              1,206,000     804,000
                              ----------  ----------
Total income assessable        1,766,000     869,000
```

**Answer:** Amina — **KSh 1,766,000**; Baraka — **KSh 869,000**. Each partner is separately assessed to tax at the applicable individual rates on this amount; the partnership itself is not a taxable person.

## 14. Self-assessment question bank (original)

**Q1.** Explain why a partnership is not a taxable person under the Income Tax Act. *(3 marks)*

*Model answer:* The Act's definition of "person" excludes a partnership for imposing tax; instead, partnership profits are computed at partnership level and assessed on each partner as their gain or profit under Section 4(b), preserving the look-through character of the partnership relationship.

**Q2.** State any three indicators that a partnership exists. *(3 marks)*

*Model answer:* A written partnership deed/agreement; joint tenancy or tenancy in common of business assets; sharing of gross receipts or profits; conduct evidencing a business carried on in common with a view to profit.

**Q3.** Distinguish an operating lease from a finance lease for tax purposes. *(4 marks)*

*Model answer:* Operating lease — lessor retains economic ownership, rentals taxed as business income, lessor claims wear and tear. Finance lease — economically a credit sale; lessee capitalises and claims wear and tear; only the interest element in the rental is taxable to the lessor.

**Q4.** Outline the key incentives available to an EPZ enterprise. *(5 marks)*

*Model answer:* 10-year corporation tax holiday; reduced corporation tax rate (illustrative 25%) for the following 10 years; exemption from WHT on dividends and other payments to non-residents during the holiday; duty and VAT exemption on imports of qualifying plant and inputs; exemption from stamp duty on relevant instruments.

**Q5.** State the four instalment tax due dates for a non-agricultural company with a calendar year of income, and the amount payable in each. *(4 marks)*

*Model answer:* 20 April, 20 June, 20 September, 20 December — each 25% of the lower of estimated current-year tax and 110% of prior-year tax.

**Q6.** Explain the purpose of compensating tax. *(3 marks)*

*Model answer:* To prevent a company distributing dividends out of untaxed profits (e.g. exempt income) from allowing shareholders to receive value that has never been taxed at corporate level; it operates as a top-up charge equalising the position with dividends paid from fully taxed profits.

**Q7.** State any three sector-specific tax adjustments applicable to a general insurance company. *(3 marks)*

*Model answer:* Adjustment for movements in the unearned premium reserve; adjustment for outstanding claims reserves (including IBNR); recognition of reinsurance recoveries; taxation of investment income of the fund at corporate rate.

**Q8.** In computing the tax of a bank, why are only specific loan-loss provisions deductible? *(3 marks)*

*Model answer:* Only provisions relating to *identified* impaired loans meet the "actual loss" test; general provisions are contingent estimates not tied to specific bad debts, so they are added back for tax and only released as deductions when the underlying loans are actually written off or specifically provided for under regulatory rules.

**Q9.** Explain why Kenya does not permit group loss relief. *(3 marks)*

*Model answer:* Each company is a separate taxable person; the Act taxes profits at the entity level and offers no statutory mechanism for surrendering losses between related companies, so a subsidiary's loss cannot offset a fellow subsidiary's or the parent's taxable profit.

**Q10.** Distinguish the tax treatment of farm works from ordinary wear and tear allowances. *(3 marks)*

*Model answer:* Farm works deduction applies to qualifying capital expenditure on farm buildings, water works, fences and land improvements, and is spread over a prescribed number of years at a statutory rate. Wear and tear applies to plant and machinery on a reducing-balance basis by asset class; the two run in parallel on different asset pools and cannot be double-claimed.

## 15. Exam technique and revision checklist

- Partnership questions: always show the **appropriation account** first (salary → interest on capital → interest on drawings → residual split), then the partner-by-partner assessment.
- Group questions: state explicitly that Kenya has **no group loss relief** and treat each company as separate.
- Farmer / EPZ / insurance / bank questions: name the **sector-specific rule** in your first sentence, then apply it — a generic corporate-tax computation loses the specialised-activity marks.
- On instalment tax: quote **both** bases (current-year estimate and 110% prior-year) and pick the lower.
- Compensating tax and EPZ holidays: flag that specific **statutory figures may have been revised** by the current Finance Act and answer using the framework the examiner has provided or the most recent published rate.
- Where the Act uses a threshold percentage (e.g. 12.5% control for inter-company dividend exemption), quote the number even if it may have changed — showing knowledge of the framework matters more than a stale number.

**Quick revision — one line per topic:**
1. Partnership = look-through; assess partners individually under Section 4(b).
2. Group companies each pay their own tax — no group loss relief in Kenya.
3. Farmers: farm-works deduction + special stock rules for livestock/produce.
4. Professionals: accrual + WIP + WHT credit; specific bad debts only.
5. Leasing: classify operating vs finance to avoid double-claiming allowances.
6. EPZ: 10-year holiday, then reduced rate, plus WHT/VAT/duty reliefs.
7. Banks: only specific loan-loss provisions deductible.
8. Insurance: ring-fence life vs general funds; adjust reserves.
9. Companies pay tax in 4 instalments (25% each) on the 20th of months 4, 6, 9, 12.
10. Compensating tax prevents untaxed-profit dividends escaping the system.
