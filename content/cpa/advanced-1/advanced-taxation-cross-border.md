## 1. Residence and cross-border taxation

**Why residence matters:**
- Tax liability in Kenya turns on *residence*, not nationality.
- Residents: taxed on worldwide income (Kenya + foreign employment income), at graduated rates.
- Non-residents: taxed only on Kenya-sourced income, often at special/flat withholding rates.

**Individual — resident if:**
- Has a permanent home in Kenya and was present for any period in the year; **or**
- No permanent home, but present ≥183 days total in the year; **or**
- No permanent home, but present in the current year *and* averaged >122 days/year over the two preceding years.

**Body of persons — resident if:**
- Incorporated under Kenyan law; **or**
- Management and control exercised in Kenya during the year; **or**
- Gazetted as resident by the Cabinet Secretary, National Treasury.

**Practical consequences of residence status:**
- Residents: graduated tax rates; some expenses/reliefs allowable; withholding tax only on select income types.
- Non-resident companies with a Kenyan branch: taxed at a higher flat corporate rate than resident companies.
- Non-resident companies with *no* branch in Kenya: withholding tax on essentially all Kenya-sourced income.
- Income accrued in or derived from Kenya is taxable regardless of residence; certain foreign-sourced business/employment income can also be pulled into charge under specific conditions.

## 2. Trading in vs trading with a country

| Concept | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Trading **in** a country | Conducting business activity *within* that country's domestic jurisdiction | A foreign company opens a branch in Kenya and operates there |
| Trading **with** a country | Cross-border commercial dealings without a domestic presence | A foreign company exports goods into Kenya without establishing a branch |

**Why the distinction matters:** "trading in" typically creates a taxable presence (a permanent establishment) subjecting local profits to Kenyan tax; "trading with" generally does not, absent a permanent establishment.

## 3. Double taxation agreements (DTAs)

**Double taxation** — the same income taxed by two or more jurisdictions, because:
- The residence country taxes worldwide income, **and**
- The source country taxes income arising within its borders, regardless of the taxpayer's residence.

**A DTA is a bilateral agreement that:**
- Allocates taxing rights between the source state and the resident state.
- Sets out which country has *priority* to tax specific income types.
- Specifies exemptions and set-off/credit mechanisms.
- Typically reduces withholding tax rates on cross-border dividends, interest, and royalties between the two states.

**Legal basis in Kenya:**
- The Income Tax Act empowers the government to negotiate double-taxation relief agreements.
- A statutory anti-treaty-shopping restriction limits DTA relief for a resident of the other contracting state **unless**: (a) more than 50% of that person's underlying ownership is held by individuals resident in that other state, **or** (b) that person is a company listed on a stock exchange in that other state.

**Two categories of relief:**

| Situation | Basis of relief |
|---|---|
| No DTA exists with the source country | Statutory unilateral relief — limited to specific income categories (e.g. employment income; income from public appearances/performances); relief = **lower of** (i) the increase in Kenyan tax caused by including the foreign income, and (ii) the actual Kenyan tax on that income |
| DTA exists with the source country | Treaty (credit method) relief — relief = **lesser of** (i) the increase in Kenyan tax caused by including the foreign income, and (ii) the foreign tax actually paid on that income |

**Effect of the credit method:** extra Kenyan tax is only payable on foreign income if the foreign tax rate was *lower* than the Kenyan rate. If the foreign rate was equal to or higher, no top-up tax is due in Kenya.

## 4. Regional trade blocs: EAC and COMESA

### East African Community (EAC)
- Members: Kenya, Uganda, Tanzania, Rwanda, Burundi (headquartered in Arusha).
- Integration proceeds in stages: **Customs Union → Common Market → Monetary Union → Political Federation.**
- Customs Union: common external tariff (CET) on imports from outside the bloc; zero internal duty on goods meeting EAC Rules of Origin; elimination of non-tariff barriers.
- Common Market: "four freedoms" — movement of goods, persons, labour, capital — plus rights of establishment and residence.
- Monetary Union: roadmap toward a single regional currency, requiring harmonised monetary/fiscal policy first.
- Political Federation: the ultimate, longer-term integration goal.

### COMESA (Common Market for Eastern and Southern Africa)
- A larger regional bloc than the EAC, spanning many Eastern/Southern African states.
- Operates a Free Trade Area: member states satisfying Rules of Origin get duty-free (or significantly reduced-duty) access to each other's markets.
- Does not permit non-tariff barriers or import bans between members.

**Exam tip:** know which stage each bloc has reached — a question may ask you to identify the *current* stage of integration, which changes over time; always state the stage as understood *at the time the question is set*, and note this in your answer if uncertain.

## 5. Most Favoured Nation (MFN) status

- A trade-policy principle: harmonising customs/excise treatment among bloc members to make intra-bloc trade more attractive than trade with outsiders.
- Uniform tariffs pursued for: welfare gains, economies of scale, increased competition, and investment flows into the region.
- MFN arrangements are a stepping stone toward deeper integration (customs union → common market → economic union).
- Examples of blocs applying MFN-style preferential treatment: EU, EAC, COMESA, ECOWAS, and others.

## 6. One-Stop Border Posts (OSBPs)

**Traditional ("double stop") border crossing:**
- Separate exit formalities in the departure country, then separate entry formalities in the destination country.
- Duplicated paperwork, inconsistent procedures between agencies, slower clearance.

**OSBP model:**
- Both exit and entry procedures performed **once**, in a single shared facility (the "Common Control Zone").
- Combines national (intra-agency) and bilateral (inter-agency) controls.

**Benefits:**
- Reduced clearance time at the border.
- Better inter-agency cooperation and information-sharing.
- Harmonised procedures between the two countries.
- Reduced congestion on trade corridors; better use of shared infrastructure (scanners, weighbridges).
- Strengthened bilateral relations between neighbouring states.

## 7. Generalised System of Preferences (GSP) and AGOA

**GSP:**
- Non-reciprocal, non-discriminatory tariff preference scheme.
- Developed ("donor") countries grant reduced or zero tariffs to developing ("beneficiary") countries' exports.
- Objectives: boost beneficiary export earnings, support industrialisation, encourage economic diversification.
- **Rules of origin** are central — preference only applies to goods genuinely produced/sufficiently transformed in the beneficiary country, consigned directly to the donor country.

**AGOA (African Growth and Opportunity Act):**
- A US programme granting duty-free access to the US market for eligible sub-Saharan African exports.
- Broader product coverage than standard US GSP (adds items like apparel, footwear, and other import-sensitive goods).
- Country eligibility depends on progress toward: market-based economic policy, rule of law, removing trade/investment barriers, IP protection, anti-corruption efforts, poverty reduction, human/worker rights protections, and eliminating exploitative child labour.
- GSP eligibility is a precondition for full AGOA benefits (including the apparel provisions).

## 8. Tax havens and treaty shopping

**Tax havens (offshore centres):**
- Jurisdictions that harmonise company, trust, banking, and tax rules specifically to attract foreign investment.
- Typical features: tax-free bank interest and dividends, very low or nil corporate tax, no capital gains tax, unrestricted profit repatriation.
- Commonly cited examples: Seychelles, Bahamas, Cyprus, Panama, British Virgin Islands, Isle of Man, Channel Islands, Liechtenstein — the exact list of jurisdictions treated as tax havens can change, so always verify current designations rather than treating any list as permanent.

**Treaty shopping:**
- Structuring ownership through a third country **solely** to access that third country's favourable DTA with the source country, even though the ultimate owner isn't genuinely resident there.
- Purpose: reduce source-country withholding tax on dividends, interest, royalties, or business profits.
- Countered by **anti-treaty-shopping (limitation-of-benefits) rules** — e.g. Kenya's ownership/listing conditions described in Section 3 above exist precisely to block this kind of structuring.

---

## 9. Tax Information Exchange Agreements (TIEA)

- Bilateral agreements enabling tax authorities to exchange taxpayer information across borders.
- Purpose: combat capital flight, money laundering, transfer mispricing, and corruption.
- Particularly aimed at uncovering offshore structures (trusts, shell companies, foundations) used to conceal income.
- Kenya has been building a TIEA network with a number of jurisdictions, though the exact list of signed/ratified agreements changes over time — verify the current status rather than assuming any past list is still accurate.

## 10. Transfer pricing and the OECD guidelines

**Why it exists:** related entities (e.g. a Kenyan subsidiary and its foreign parent) can shift profit out of Kenya by mispricing intercompany transactions. Transfer pricing rules require such transactions to reflect an **arm's length price** — the price independent, unrelated parties would agree.

**When the rules apply:**
- Transactions between associated enterprises of a multinational, where one party is taxable in Kenya and the other is outside Kenya.
- Transactions between a permanent establishment and its head office/other related branches (the PE is treated as a distinct, separate enterprise for this purpose).

**When enterprises are "related":**
- One participates (directly/indirectly) in the management, control, or capital of the other, **or** a third party does so for both; **or**
- Individuals controlling each enterprise are connected by marriage, blood relation (consanguinity), or affinity.

**OECD-recognised transfer pricing methods:**

*Traditional transaction methods:*
- Comparable Uncontrolled Price (CUP) method
- Resale price method
- Cost-plus method

*Transactional profit methods:*
- Transactional net margin method (TNMM)
- Transactional profit split method

**Selecting a method:** taxpayers choose the *most appropriate* method given the transaction's nature — the "best" method is the one that most closely approximates the arm's length result, using the most reliable data, with the fewest adjustments.

**Documentation should cover:**
- Method selected and reasoning.
- How the method was applied (calculations, adjustment factors).
- The enterprise's global organisational structure.
- Details of the specific transaction(s).
- Assumptions, strategies, and policies applied.

**Why documentation matters (benefits of a transfer pricing study):**
- Shifts the burden of proof toward the position being defensible.
- Can reduce/avoid penalty exposure.
- Reduces overall tax risk and audit exposure.
- Supports consistent treatment across direct and indirect taxes (e.g. customs valuation).

**Consequences of an adjustment:** where the tax authority adjusts profits under the arm's length rule and additional tax becomes due, that additional tax is generally subject to the same late-payment penalty and interest regime as any other unpaid tax — always check the current penalty/interest rates, since these are revised periodically by Finance Acts.

## 11. Case law: the role of OECD guidance in Kenyan practice

- Kenyan courts have, in reported tax litigation, accepted that **where domestic legislation does not itself prescribe a detailed methodology** for determining an arm's length price, it is reasonable for a taxpayer (and for the tribunal reviewing an assessment) to have regard to internationally recognised guidance — such as the OECD Transfer Pricing Guidelines — as a proper basis for that determination, provided the taxpayer has not engaged in fraudulent structuring aimed at evading tax.
- The broader principle illustrated: a tax authority seeking to reassess a taxpayer's cross-border pricing bears the burden of establishing, with reasonable clarity, that the pricing was **not** at arm's length before a higher tax charge can be sustained.
- **Exam tip:** when citing case law from memory, state the general legal principle clearly even if you are unsure of the exact case name, citation, or year — examiners generally award marks for correctly identifying and applying the *principle*, and a mis-cited or omitted case name loses far fewer marks than a wrong or absent principle.

## 12. Full worked question — double taxation relief

**Scenario (original):** Wanjiku, a resident of Kenya, earned the following income for the year ended 31 December 2026:

- Income from Kenya: KSh 1,900,000
- Income from Country X (a DTA exists between Kenya and Country X): FC 6,000 gross, of which FC 900 was withheld as tax in Country X. Average exchange rate for the year: 1 FC = KSh 130.

*(Illustrative tax bands used for this example only — always use the actual current bands in your exam):* first KSh 150,000 @ 10%; next KSh 150,000 @ 15%; next KSh 150,000 @ 20%; next KSh 150,000 @ 25%; remainder @ 30%.

**Required:** Compute the double taxation relief due to Wanjiku for the year.

**Model answer**

*Step 1 — convert foreign income and tax to KES*
```
Gross foreign income = FC 6,000 × 130   = KSh 780,000
Foreign tax paid     = FC 900 × 130     = KSh 117,000
```

*Step 2 — total income*
```
Kenya income                = 1,900,000
Foreign income (gross)      =   780,000
                               ---------
Total income                 = 2,680,000
```

*Step 3 — tax on total income*
```
First   150,000 @ 10% =  15,000
Next    150,000 @ 15% =  22,500
Next    150,000 @ 20% =  30,000
Next    150,000 @ 25% =  37,500
Remainder 2,080,000 @ 30% = 624,000
                          ---------
Tax on total income        729,000
```

*Step 4 — tax on Kenya income only*
```
First   150,000 @ 10% =  15,000
Next    150,000 @ 15% =  22,500
Next    150,000 @ 20% =  30,000
Next    150,000 @ 25% =  37,500
Remainder 1,300,000 @ 30% = 390,000
                          ---------
Tax on Kenya-only income    495,000
```

*Step 5 — relief*
```
Increase in tax due to foreign income = 729,000 − 495,000 = 234,000
Foreign tax paid (KES)                = 117,000

Relief = lesser of (234,000, 117,000) = KSh 117,000
```

**Answer:** Double taxation relief due to Wanjiku = **KSh 117,000**. Since the foreign tax paid is lower than the additional Kenyan tax the foreign income would otherwise trigger, the *full* foreign tax paid is creditable — Wanjiku still pays the balancing 234,000 − 117,000 = KSh 117,000 in additional Kenyan tax on the foreign income (i.e. Kenya "tops up" the difference between the two rates).

## 13. Self-assessment question bank (original questions, with model answers)

**Q1.** State the three conditions under which an individual is treated as resident in Kenya for tax purposes. *(3 marks)*

*Model answer:*
- Has a permanent home in Kenya and was present for any period in the year; **or**
- No permanent home, but present ≥183 days total in the year; **or**
- No permanent home, but present in the current year and averaged >122 days/year over the two preceding years.

**Q2.** Distinguish "trading in" a country from "trading with" a country. *(3 marks)*

*Model answer:*
- Trading **in**: conducting business activity within that country's own jurisdiction (e.g. operating a branch there) — typically creates a taxable presence.
- Trading **with**: cross-border dealings (e.g. exporting goods) without establishing a domestic presence — generally does not, absent a permanent establishment.

**Q3.** Explain the "credit method" of double taxation relief under a DTA. *(3 marks)*

*Model answer:*
- Relief = the **lesser of**: (i) the increase in Kenyan tax caused by including the foreign income, and (ii) the foreign tax actually paid.
- Effect: extra Kenyan tax is only due if the foreign rate was *lower* than Kenya's rate.
- If the foreign rate was equal to or higher, no top-up Kenyan tax arises.

**Q4.** State the four stages of EAC regional integration, in order. *(4 marks)*

*Model answer:*
- Customs Union
- Common Market
- Monetary Union
- Political Federation

**Q5.** Explain the purpose of "Rules of Origin" in a preferential trade scheme such as GSP. *(3 marks)*

*Model answer:*
- Ensure preferential (reduced/zero) tariff treatment applies **only** to goods genuinely produced or sufficiently transformed in the beneficiary country.
- Prevent a third country from routing goods through a beneficiary country merely to capture the preference ("origin laundering").
- Typically require direct consignment from the beneficiary country to the donor country as supporting evidence.

**Q6.** State three benefits of a One-Stop Border Post compared with the traditional "double stop" model. *(3 marks)*

*Model answer:* (any three)
- Reduced clearance time.
- Improved inter-agency cooperation.
- Harmonised procedures between the two countries.
- Reduced corridor congestion / better use of shared infrastructure.

**Q7.** Define "treaty shopping" and state one method used to counter it. *(3 marks)*

*Model answer:*
- Treaty shopping: structuring ownership through a third country solely to access that country's favourable DTA with the source country, without genuine residence there.
- Countered by: limitation-of-benefits / anti-treaty-shopping rules — e.g. requiring a minimum level of genuine local ownership, or listing on a local stock exchange, before DTA relief is available.

**Q8.** List the five OECD-recognised transfer pricing methods, grouped by category. *(5 marks)*

*Model answer:*
- Traditional transaction methods: Comparable Uncontrolled Price (CUP); Resale price method; Cost-plus method.
- Transactional profit methods: Transactional net margin method (TNMM); Transactional profit split method.

**Q9.** State two circumstances under which two enterprises are treated as "related" for transfer pricing purposes. *(2 marks)*

*Model answer:* (any two)
- One enterprise participates directly/indirectly in the management, control, or capital of the other.
- A third party participates directly/indirectly in the management, control, or capital of both enterprises.
- Individuals controlling each enterprise are connected by marriage, blood relation, or affinity.

**Q10.** Explain the purpose of a Tax Information Exchange Agreement (TIEA). *(3 marks)*

*Model answer:*
- Enables tax authorities in different jurisdictions to exchange taxpayer information.
- Targets capital flight, money laundering, transfer mispricing, and corruption.
- Particularly useful for uncovering offshore structures (trusts, shell companies) used to hide income or facilitate tax fraud.

## 14. Exam technique and revision checklist

- DTA-relief questions: **always convert foreign figures to KES first**, then compute tax on total income and on Kenya-only income separately, then take the **lesser of** the two relief tests.
- Regional-bloc questions: state the **stage of integration** explicitly (Customs Union / Common Market / Monetary Union / Political Federation) — vague answers about "cooperation" without naming the stage lose marks.
- Transfer pricing questions: always **name the method used and justify why it's the most appropriate** for the transaction type, not just describe the arm's length principle in the abstract.
- Case-law questions: prioritise stating the **legal principle correctly** over getting an exact citation right — an unnamed but correctly explained principle scores far better than a confidently wrong citation.
- Never assert specific tax rates, thresholds, or treaty-country lists as certain in the exam without first checking the current Finance Act and KRA guidance — these change yearly.

**Quick revision — one line per topic:**
1. Residence (not nationality) determines the scope of Kenyan tax liability.
2. "Trading in" typically creates a taxable presence; "trading with" generally does not.
3. DTA relief (credit method) = lesser of (extra Kenyan tax from foreign income) and (foreign tax paid).
4. EAC integration: Customs Union → Common Market → Monetary Union → Political Federation.
5. COMESA is a larger regional bloc offering duty-free/reduced-duty access under its Free Trade Area.
6. OSBPs combine exit and entry formalities into one facility, cutting clearance time.
7. GSP/AGOA extend preferential market access to developing/African exporters, gated by Rules of Origin and eligibility criteria.
8. Tax havens harmonise company/trust/tax law specifically to attract foreign capital.
9. Treaty shopping is countered by limitation-of-benefits rules tied to genuine local ownership.
