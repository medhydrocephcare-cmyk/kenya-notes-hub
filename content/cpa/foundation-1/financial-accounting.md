## 1. Introduction to accounting and the regulatory framework

### 1.1 What accounting is

Accounting is the process of **identifying, recording, classifying, summarising, and communicating** financial information about an economic entity to people who use that information to make decisions.

Three stages happen in sequence:

| Stage | What happens | Output |
|---|---|---|
| Recording (bookkeeping) | Transactions are captured as they occur | Books of original entry, ledgers |
| Classifying & summarising | Similar items are grouped and totalled | Trial balance |
| Communicating | Summarised data is presented for decisions | Financial statements, reports |

### 1.2 Users of accounting information and their needs

| User | Typical question they want answered |
|---|---|
| Owners/shareholders | Is my investment growing? Should I keep, add, or withdraw capital? |
| Management | Are we hitting targets? Where should resources go next period? |
| Lenders/banks | Can this entity service debt? Is security adequate? |
| Suppliers (trade creditors) | Will I be paid on time if I extend credit? |
| Customers | Will this supplier still be operating to honour warranties/contracts? |
| Government (KRA) | What tax is due? Is the entity complying with tax law? |
| Employees | Is my job secure? Can the business afford a pay rise? |
| The public | Does the entity provide local employment, pay fairly, operate responsibly? |

### 1.3 Branches of accounting

- **Financial accounting** — historical, external-facing, governed by IFRS/IAS, produces the statement of profit or loss, statement of financial position, statement of cash flows.
- **Management accounting** — internal-facing, forward-looking, no mandatory format (budgets, variance analysis, costing).
- **Cost accounting** — a subset of management accounting focused on determining the cost of products/services.
- **Auditing** — independent examination of financial statements to give an opinion on whether they are true and fair.
- **Tax accounting** — computing tax liabilities in line with tax law (in Kenya, governed by the Income Tax Act and administered by KRA).

### 1.4 Regulatory and conceptual framework

- **IASB (International Accounting Standards Board)** issues **IFRS** (International Financial Reporting Standards) and **IAS** (older International Accounting Standards, still in force unless superseded).
- In Kenya, the **Institute of Certified Public Accountants of Kenya (ICPAK)** requires entities to prepare financial statements in accordance with IFRS, and the **Companies Act, 2015** sets statutory reporting requirements for registered companies.
- The **IASB Conceptual Framework** underpins all standards. Its key ideas at Foundation level:
  - **Underlying assumption:** going concern — the entity is assumed to continue operating for the foreseeable future.
  - **Qualitative characteristics:** relevance, faithful representation (fundamental); comparability, verifiability, timeliness, understandability (enhancing).
  - **Elements of financial statements:** asset, liability, equity, income, expense.

### 1.5 Accounting concepts and conventions

| Concept | Meaning | Quick example |
|---|---|---|
| Business entity | The business is accounted for separately from its owner | The owner's personal car is not a business asset unless brought into the business |
| Money measurement | Only transactions expressible in money are recorded | Staff morale is not recorded; a salary payment is |
| Historical cost | Assets are initially recorded at original purchase cost | Land bought for KSh 2,000,000 stays at that cost, even if market value rises, until a revaluation policy is applied |
| Accruals (matching) | Income and expenses are recognised when earned/incurred, not when cash moves | Rent invoiced in June but paid in July is a June expense |
| Consistency | The same accounting policy is applied period to period | If straight-line depreciation is chosen, it should not be swapped every year without justification |
| Prudence | Do not overstate assets/income or understate liabilities/expenses | Provide for a doubtful debt even before it is confirmed bad |
| Going concern | The entity will continue operating | Assets are valued as "in use," not at forced-sale/liquidation values |
| Materiality | Only information that could influence a user's decision needs separate disclosure | A KSh 50 stationery item is expensed immediately, not capitalised and depreciated |
| Dual aspect | Every transaction has two effects on the accounting equation | Buying a motor vehicle for cash increases one asset and decreases another |

**Memory aid:** *"BEAM-HP-CG-D"* — Business entity, Historical cost, Accruals, Materiality, Historical... (build your own acronym from the concepts in the order you find easiest to recall; the point is to have all nine ready to name and illustrate in an exam answer).

## 2. The accounting equation and double entry

### 2.1 The accounting equation

```
ASSETS = LIABILITIES + CAPITAL (EQUITY)
```

Every transaction keeps this equation in balance. Capital itself expands to:

```
Capital = Opening capital + Profit (or − Loss) + Additional capital introduced − Drawings
```

### 2.2 Double entry rules

Every transaction is recorded **twice** — once as a debit, once as a credit, of equal value.

| Account type | Increase | Decrease |
|---|---|---|
| Asset | Debit | Credit |
| Expense | Debit | Credit |
| Drawings | Debit | Credit |
| Liability | Credit | Debit |
| Capital/Equity | Credit | Debit |
| Income | Credit | Debit |

**Mnemonic — DEAD CLIC:** *Debit: Expenses, Assets, Drawings. Credit: Liabilities, Income, Capital.*

### 2.3 Worked example

Peter starts a stationery shop on 1 March 2026.

| # | Transaction | Debit | Credit |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Peter deposits KSh 500,000 as capital into the business bank account | Bank 500,000 | Capital 500,000 |
| 2 | Buys shop fittings for cash, KSh 80,000 | Fittings 80,000 | Bank 80,000 |
| 3 | Buys inventory on credit from Rift Wholesalers, KSh 150,000 | Purchases 150,000 | Rift Wholesalers (payable) 150,000 |
| 4 | Sells goods for cash, KSh 60,000 (cost KSh 40,000) | Bank 60,000 | Sales 60,000 |
| 5 | Pays Rift Wholesalers KSh 100,000 on account | Rift Wholesalers 100,000 | Bank 100,000 |
| 6 | Peter withdraws KSh 20,000 for personal use | Drawings 20,000 | Bank 20,000 |

Note transaction 4: the cost of the goods sold (KSh 40,000) is *not* posted as a separate double entry under the periodic inventory system used at Foundation level — it is accounted for through the purchases and closing inventory figures when the statement of profit or loss is prepared (see Section 12).

## 3. Books of original entry

Books of original (or "prime") entry are where transactions are first recorded, before being posted to the ledger.

| Book | Records | Posted to |
|---|---|---|
| Sales day book | Credit sales (per invoice issued) | Debit each customer's account; credit Sales account in total |
| Purchases day book | Credit purchases | Credit each supplier's account; debit Purchases account in total |
| Sales returns day book | Goods returned by customers | Credit each customer; debit Sales Returns |
| Purchases returns day book | Goods returned to suppliers | Debit each supplier; credit Purchases Returns |
| Cash book | All cash and bank receipts/payments | Dual-purpose — acts as both a book of prime entry and a ledger account |
| Petty cash book | Small, low-value cash expenses, usually run on the **imprest system** | Individual expense accounts (analysis columns) |
| General journal | Anything that doesn't fit the above: opening entries, non-current asset purchases/disposals, correction of errors, year-end adjustments | Relevant ledger accounts |

### 3.1 The imprest system (petty cash)

A fixed **"float"** (e.g. KSh 10,000) is given to the petty cashier. At the end of the period, the cashier is reimbursed exactly the amount spent, restoring the float back to KSh 10,000.

*Worked example:* Float = KSh 10,000. During the month, KSh 7,400 is spent (KSh 3,000 stationery, KSh 2,500 tea/refreshments, KSh 1,900 transport). Reimbursement at month end = KSh 7,400, restoring the float to KSh 10,000 for the next period.

## 4. Ledger accounts and the trial balance

### 4.1 The ledger

The ledger is where postings from books of prime entry accumulate account-by-account. Traditionally split into:

- **Sales (debtors') ledger** — individual customer accounts.
- **Purchases (creditors') ledger** — individual supplier accounts.
- **General (nominal) ledger** — all other accounts: assets, liabilities, capital, income, expenses.
- **Cash book** — often kept as part of the general ledger.

### 4.2 Balancing an account (T-account format)

```
                    Bank Account
Dr                                                Cr
2026                          2026
Mar 1  Capital     500,000    Mar 1  Fittings      80,000
Mar 4  Sales        60,000    Mar 5  Rift Wholesalers 100,000
                               Mar 6  Drawings       20,000
                               Mar 31 Balance c/d   360,000
       ----------                    ----------
        560,000                       560,000
Apr 1  Balance b/d 360,000
```

### 4.3 The trial balance

A trial balance lists every ledger account's closing balance in a debit or credit column. If double entry has been applied correctly, **total debits = total credits**.

**What a trial balance does *not* prove:** it does not prove there are no errors — only that debits equal credits in total. Six classic errors that a trial balance will **not** reveal:

1. **Error of omission** — a transaction left out entirely.
2. **Error of commission** — posted to the correct type of account but the wrong account (e.g. one customer's account instead of another).
3. **Error of principle** — posted to the wrong class of account (e.g. a repair expensed as a non-current asset).
4. **Compensating errors** — two unrelated errors of equal and opposite value cancel out.
5. **Error of original entry** — the wrong amount is entered in both the debit and credit, e.g. KSh 5,400 recorded as KSh 4,500 in both accounts.
6. **Complete reversal of entries** — debit and credit are swapped for a correct amount.

## 5. Value Added Tax (VAT) in the accounting records

In Kenya, VAT is administered by the **Kenya Revenue Authority (KRA)** under the VAT Act. A VAT-registered business charges **output VAT** on sales and recovers **input VAT** on qualifying purchases; the difference is remitted to (or reclaimed from) KRA.

### 5.1 Recording VAT

- Sales are recorded **net of VAT**; VAT charged is credited to a **VAT (output) account**.
- Purchases are recorded **net of VAT**; VAT paid is debited to the same VAT control account.
- The net balance on the VAT account at period end is a **liability to KRA** (if output exceeds input) or a **recoverable amount** (if input exceeds output).

*Worked example:* Standard VAT rate assumed at **16%** for illustration. A business sells goods for KSh 116,000 inclusive of VAT.

```
VAT-exclusive sales value = 116,000 × 100/116 = 100,000
VAT (output) = 116,000 × 16/116 = 16,000
```

Journal: Debit Cash/Debtors 116,000; Credit Sales 100,000; Credit VAT Output 16,000.

> Always check the VAT rate quoted in the exam question — rates are set by law and can change; do not assume a fixed percentage without reading the question.

## 6. Non-current assets and depreciation

### 6.1 Recognition and cost

A non-current asset is recognised at cost, which includes purchase price plus **all costs necessary to bring the asset to its location and working condition** (delivery, installation, non-refundable taxes), but excludes financing costs and costs of abnormal wastage.

### 6.2 Depreciation methods

| Method | Formula | When typically used |
|---|---|---|
| Straight-line | (Cost − Residual value) ÷ Useful life | Assets that wear evenly over time, e.g. buildings, fittings |
| Reducing (declining) balance | Net book value × fixed % | Assets that lose more value early (e.g. motor vehicles, computers) |
| Units of production | (Cost − Residual value) ÷ Total expected output × units produced in period | Machinery where wear tracks usage, not time |

**Worked example (straight-line):** Machine cost KSh 900,000, residual value KSh 100,000, useful life 8 years.
```
Annual depreciation = (900,000 − 100,000) ÷ 8 = KSh 100,000 per year
```

**Worked example (reducing balance):** Motor vehicle cost KSh 1,200,000, depreciated at 25% reducing balance.
```
Year 1: 1,200,000 × 25% = 300,000 → NBV 900,000
Year 2: 900,000 × 25% = 225,000 → NBV 675,000
Year 3: 675,000 × 25% = 168,750 → NBV 506,250
```

### 6.3 Disposal of a non-current asset

Steps: (1) remove the asset's original cost, (2) remove accumulated depreciation to date, (3) record sale proceeds, (4) the balancing figure on the disposal account is a **profit or loss on disposal**, taken to the statement of profit or loss.

*Worked example:* Equipment cost KSh 400,000, accumulated depreciation KSh 250,000 (NBV KSh 150,000), sold for KSh 180,000 cash.
```
Disposal account:
Dr Cost                400,000    Cr Accumulated depreciation   250,000
                                   Cr Bank (proceeds)            180,000
                                   Cr Profit on disposal          30,000
                        400,000                                 460,000... 
```
Check: 250,000 + 180,000 = 430,000 credits vs 400,000 debit cost → profit on disposal = 430,000 − 400,000 = **KSh 30,000 profit**.

## 7. Inventory (stock) valuation

### 7.1 The rule

Per **IAS 2**, inventory is valued at the **lower of cost and net realisable value (NRV)**, applied item by item (or group of similar items).

```
NRV = Estimated selling price − estimated costs to complete − estimated costs to sell
```

### 7.2 Cost formulas

| Method | How it works |
|---|---|
| FIFO (first in, first out) | Oldest costs are assumed sold first; closing inventory is valued at the most recent purchase costs |
| Weighted average cost (AVCO) | A new average cost is recalculated after each purchase (or periodically); closing inventory valued at the latest average |

**Worked example (FIFO):**

| Date | Transaction | Units | Unit cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 Jun | Opening inventory | 100 | 50 |
| 10 Jun | Purchase | 200 | 55 |
| 20 Jun | Sale | 150 | — |

Under FIFO, the 150 units sold are assumed to come from the 100 opening units (@50) first, then 50 of the June-10 batch (@55).
```
Closing inventory = 150 units of the June-10 batch remaining × 55 = KSh 8,250
```

### 7.3 Why the choice matters

In a period of rising prices, FIFO produces a **higher** closing inventory value and **higher** reported profit than AVCO, because FIFO leaves the most recent (higher) costs in closing inventory while AVCO smooths costs across all units.

## 8. Accruals and prepayments

Applying the accruals concept: expenses/income are matched to the period they relate to, regardless of when cash moves.

| Situation | Adjustment |
|---|---|
| Expense incurred but not yet paid at period end | **Accrued expense** — add to the expense in the statement of profit or loss; show as a current liability |
| Expense paid in advance, relating to next period | **Prepayment** — deduct from the expense; show as a current asset |
| Income earned but not yet received | **Accrued income** — add to income; show as a current asset |
| Income received in advance, relating to next period | **Income received in advance (deferred income)** — deduct from income; show as a current liability |

**Worked example:** Rent expense account shows cash paid during the year of KSh 240,000. Rent for December (KSh 20,000) has not yet been paid at year end.
```
Rent expense for the year (SPL) = 240,000 + 20,000 accrued = KSh 260,000
Statement of financial position: Accrued expenses (current liability) = KSh 20,000
```

## 9. Irrecoverable debts and allowances for doubtful debts

- **Irrecoverable (bad) debt** — a specific debt written off because it is confirmed uncollectible. Entry: Debit Irrecoverable Debts Expense; Credit Trade Receivables (the specific customer).
- **Allowance for doubtful debts** — a general provision against receivables that *might* not be collected, applying prudence. It does not remove a specific customer's balance; it is shown as a deduction from total trade receivables in the statement of financial position.

**Worked example:** Trade receivables at year end = KSh 800,000. A specific debt of KSh 30,000 is written off as irrecoverable. The business then wants a 5% allowance for doubtful debts on the remaining receivables.
```
Receivables after write-off = 800,000 − 30,000 = 770,000
Allowance required = 770,000 × 5% = 38,500

Statement of financial position (extract):
Trade receivables            770,000
Less: allowance for doubtful debts  (38,500)
                              738,500 (net receivables)
```
If an allowance already existed from the prior year, only the **increase or decrease** in the allowance is charged/credited to the statement of profit or loss for the current year — not the full new balance.

## 10. Bank reconciliation statements

Differences between the cash book balance and the bank statement balance arise from **timing differences** and **errors**.

| Item | Cause |
|---|---|
| Unpresented cheques | Cheques written and recorded in the cash book, not yet cleared by the bank |
| Uncredited deposits (lodgements) | Money paid in and recorded in the cash book, not yet reflected on the bank statement |
| Bank charges / interest | Often appear on the bank statement before the business records them |
| Standing orders / direct debits | Automatic payments the bank processes that the business may not yet have recorded |
| Dishonoured cheques | A customer's cheque that bounced — needs to be reversed in the cash book |

**Worked example:**
```
Cash book balance (updated for bank charges of 1,500 and a dishonoured cheque of 12,000 not yet recorded): to find

Balance per bank statement                     245,000
Add: uncredited deposits                        30,000
Less: unpresented cheques                      (18,000)
= Balance per cash book (corrected)            257,000
```
Always reconcile **from the bank statement balance to the corrected cash book balance** (or vice versa) — never mix uncorrected cash book figures with bank statement adjustments.

## 11. Control accounts, errors, and suspense accounts

### 11.1 Control accounts

A **sales ledger control account** (total receivables account) and **purchases ledger control account** (total payables account) summarise, in one place, everything that individually appears across all the customer/supplier accounts in the subsidiary ledgers. Its closing balance should agree with the total of individual balances in the sales/purchases ledger — this is used to check the accuracy of the ledgers.

**Typical entries in a sales ledger control account:**

| Debit side | Credit side |
|---|---|
| Opening balance | Cash/cheques received from customers |
| Credit sales (from sales day book) | Sales returns |
| Dishonoured cheques | Discounts allowed |
| | Irrecoverable debts written off |
| | Closing balance |

### 11.2 Errors and the suspense account

When a trial balance does not balance, the difference is temporarily posted to a **suspense account** so that draft financial statements can still be prepared while the error is traced.

**Worked example:** Trial balance credit column exceeds debit column by KSh 4,000. On investigation: a purchase of KSh 4,000 was correctly entered in the Purchases account but never posted to the supplier's account.

```
Journal: Dr Suspense 4,000 ; Cr Supplier's account 4,000
```
This clears the suspense account and corrects the omitted credit entry.

Only errors that cause the trial balance **not to balance** pass through a suspense account (e.g. one-sided entries, or entries of different amounts on each side). Errors of omission, commission, principle, original entry (both sides equal but wrong figure), complete reversal, and compensating errors do **not** go through suspense because the trial balance still balances despite them (see Section 4.3).

## 13. Financial statements of not-for-profit organisations

Clubs, societies, and similar not-for-profit entities use different terminology but the same accruals logic:

| For-profit term | Not-for-profit equivalent |
|---|---|
| Statement of profit or loss | Income and expenditure account |
| Profit | Surplus |
| Loss | Deficit |
| Capital | Accumulated fund |
| Cash receipts/payments record | Receipts and payments account |

**Subscriptions** are a common Foundation-level trap: subscriptions *received* in cash during the year must be adjusted for amounts owing/prepaid at the start and end of the year to arrive at subscriptions *earned* (income) for the period, using the same accrual logic as Section 8.

## 14. Introduction to company accounts

At Foundation level, the focus is on the basic structure rather than full IFRS-compliant published accounts:

- A company's equity is divided into **share capital** (ordinary/preference shares at nominal value) and **reserves** (e.g. retained earnings, share premium).
- **Dividends** are a distribution of profit to shareholders, not an expense in arriving at profit — they reduce retained earnings directly.
- Unlike a sole trader, a company is a **separate legal entity**, so "drawings" as a concept does not apply to shareholders; only formally declared dividends reduce equity.

## 15. Full worked revision question

**Scenario (original):** Amina runs a retail shop as a sole trader. The following balances were extracted from her books at 31 December 2026, before adjustments:

| Account | KSh |
|---|---|
| Sales | 2,400,000 |
| Purchases | 1,500,000 |
| Opening inventory (1 Jan 2026) | 180,000 |
| Rent | 240,000 |
| Wages | 360,000 |
| Trade receivables | 300,000 |
| Trade payables | 210,000 |
| Bank | 95,000 (debit) |
| Fittings, at cost | 400,000 |
| Accumulated depreciation on fittings (1 Jan 2026) | 120,000 |
| Capital (1 Jan 2026) | 495,000 |
| Drawings | 150,000 |

Additional information:
1. Closing inventory at 31 December 2026 was valued at KSh 210,000.
2. Rent of KSh 20,000 for December 2026 was unpaid at year end (accrual).
3. Fittings are depreciated at 10% per annum on cost, straight-line.
4. Trade receivables include an irrecoverable debt of KSh 15,000 to be written off; an allowance for doubtful debts of 5% is then required on the remaining balance.

**Required:** Prepare the statement of profit or loss for the year ended 31 December 2026 and the statement of financial position as at that date.

**Model answer:**

*Step 1 — irrecoverable debt and allowance*
```
Receivables after write-off = 300,000 − 15,000 = 285,000
Allowance for doubtful debts = 285,000 × 5% = 14,250
Irrecoverable debts expense (SPL) = 15,000 + 14,250 = 29,250
```

*Step 2 — depreciation*
```
Depreciation for the year = 400,000 × 10% = 40,000
Accumulated depreciation c/f = 120,000 + 40,000 = 160,000
```

*Step 3 — rent expense*
```
Rent expense (SPL) = 240,000 + 20,000 accrual = 260,000
```

**Statement of profit or loss for the year ended 31 December 2026**


## 16. Self-assessment question bank (original questions, with model answers)

**Q1.** Define the "accruals concept" and give one example of its application other than rent or subscriptions. *(3 marks)*

*Model answer:* The accruals (matching) concept requires income and expenses to be recognised in the period they are earned or incurred, not necessarily when cash is received or paid, so that profit reflects economic activity rather than cash movement. Example: electricity used in December but billed and paid in January is charged as a December expense with a corresponding accrual at the December year end.

**Q2.** A trader bought a delivery van for KSh 1,800,000 on 1 January 2025. It is depreciated at 20% per annum on the reducing balance method. Calculate the net book value at 31 December 2027 (three full years). *(4 marks)*

*Model answer:*
```
End of 2025: 1,800,000 × (1 − 0.20) = 1,440,000
End of 2026: 1,440,000 × 0.80 = 1,152,000
End of 2027: 1,152,000 × 0.80 = 921,600
```
Net book value at 31 December 2027 = **KSh 921,600**.

**Q3.** Explain, with a journal entry, how an irrecoverable debt of KSh 12,000 from customer "Juma" is written off. *(3 marks)*

*Model answer:* Dr Irrecoverable debts expense 12,000; Cr Trade receivables — Juma 12,000. This removes the specific customer balance from receivables and charges the loss to the statement of profit or loss.

**Q4.** A business's cash book shows a balance of KSh 84,000. The bank statement shows KSh 101,000. On comparison, unpresented cheques total KSh 25,000 and there is an uncredited deposit of KSh 8,000. Prepare the reconciliation. *(5 marks)*

*Model answer:*
```
Balance per bank statement          101,000
Add: uncredited deposit               8,000
Less: unpresented cheques           (25,000)
                                     --------
Balance per cash book                84,000
```
This agrees with the cash book balance given, confirming no further adjustment is required.

**Q5.** State two errors that would cause a trial balance to disagree, and two that would not. *(4 marks)*

*Model answer:* Would cause disagreement: (i) a one-sided entry (posted as a debit only, with no corresponding credit); (ii) unequal amounts posted on the debit and credit sides of the same transaction. Would not cause disagreement: (i) error of complete reversal of entries; (ii) error of principle (e.g. capital expenditure treated as revenue expenditure).

**Q6.** Explain the difference between FIFO and weighted average cost as inventory valuation methods, and state the effect on profit of using FIFO during a period of rising prices. *(4 marks)*

*Model answer:* FIFO assumes the oldest inventory costs are used up first, leaving closing inventory valued at the most recent (higher, in a period of rising prices) costs. Weighted average recalculates an average cost across all units held, smoothing price changes. During rising prices, FIFO produces a higher closing inventory value and therefore a lower cost of sales, resulting in higher reported gross profit compared with weighted average.

**Q7.** A club's receipts and payments account shows subscriptions received of KSh 540,000 during the year. At the start of the year, KSh 30,000 was owed by members; at the end of the year, KSh 45,000 was owed by members and KSh 10,000 had been received in advance for next year. Calculate subscription income for the income and expenditure account. *(5 marks)*

*Model answer:*
```
Subscriptions received (cash)                     540,000
Less: opening amount owing (already counted last year) (30,000)
Add: closing amount owing (earned this year, not yet received) 45,000
Less: received in advance (relates to next year)       (10,000)
                                                        --------
Subscription income for the year                       545,000
```

**Q8.** Distinguish between capital expenditure and revenue expenditure, giving one example of each in the context of a delivery business. *(3 marks)*

*Model answer:* Capital expenditure creates or enhances a non-current asset with benefits lasting more than one accounting period, e.g. purchasing a new delivery truck. Revenue expenditure is incurred in the day-to-day running of the business and is consumed within the period, e.g. fuel for the delivery trucks.

**Q9.** A supplier's statement shows a balance owing of KSh 95,000, but the trader's purchases ledger shows KSh 83,000 owing to the same supplier. On investigation, an invoice for KSh 12,000 had been received from the supplier but not yet entered in the trader's books. Reconcile the two figures. *(4 marks)*

*Model answer:*
```
Balance per trader's ledger              83,000
Add: invoice not yet recorded            12,000
                                          -------
Corrected balance (agrees with supplier's statement)  95,000
```

**Q10.** Briefly explain the "prudence" concept and how it is applied when accounting for an allowance for doubtful debts. *(3 marks)*

*Model answer:* Prudence requires that assets and income are not overstated and liabilities and expenses are not understated, so that financial statements do not present an unduly optimistic picture. Applied to receivables, a business estimates the portion of trade receivables that may not be collected and creates an allowance for doubtful debts, reducing the net receivables shown as an asset and charging the estimated loss against profit, even before any specific debt is confirmed irrecoverable.

## 17. Exam technique and revision checklist

- Always **show workings** — Foundation-level marking awards method marks even if a final figure is wrong.
- **Label every T-account and statement clearly** (Dr/Cr, account name, period covered).
- When a trial balance doesn't balance in a computational question, **state the amount of the difference and where you have posted it to suspense** before attempting to trace the error.
- For depreciation questions, check **which method** and **whether depreciation is charged in the year of disposal** — read the question's policy statement carefully; conventions vary (full year, none in year of disposal, pro-rata) and the question will specify which applies.
- For adjustment questions (accruals/prepayments/allowances), write the **formula first**, then substitute numbers — this makes errors easy to spot and easy for an examiner to award partial credit.
- Before submitting a statement of financial position, **foot (add up) both sides** and confirm they are equal — if not, re-check every adjustment in order rather than guessing.

**Quick revision summary — one line per topic:**

1. Accounting equation: Assets = Liabilities + Capital.
2. Double entry: DEAD CLIC (Debit Expenses/Assets/Drawings; Credit Liabilities/Income/Capital).
3. Books of prime entry feed the ledgers; the cash book is both prime entry and a ledger account.
4. A trial balance proves arithmetic equality, not the absence of errors.
5. VAT is recorded net of tax; the VAT account nets to a payable or recoverable balance.
6. Depreciation systematically allocates cost (less residual value) over useful life — it does not represent market value.
7. Inventory is valued at the lower of cost and net realisable value.
8. Accruals/prepayments adjust cash-based figures to the correct period's expense/income.
9. Irrecoverable debts are written off specifically; allowances for doubtful debts are a general, prudence-driven provision.
10. Bank reconciliations explain timing differences between the cash book and the bank statement.
11. Control accounts cross-check the accuracy of subsidiary ledgers; suspense accounts hold the trial balance difference until errors are traced.
12–14. Sole trader, not-for-profit, and company financial statements all rest on the same accruals-based logic, with different terminology and equity structures.
