## 1. What is economics? Scarcity, choice and opportunity cost

**Economics** is the study of how individuals, businesses and governments allocate scarce resources among competing, unlimited wants. Because resources are limited while wants are effectively unlimited, every choice involves an **opportunity cost** — the value of the next best alternative given up. **Microeconomics** studies individual consumers, firms and markets; **macroeconomics** studies the economy as a whole.

---

## 2. Demand and supply

The **law of demand**: as price rises, quantity demanded falls (all else equal), giving a downward-sloping demand curve. Non-price determinants of demand include income, tastes, prices of substitutes/complements, and expectations. The **law of supply**: as price rises, quantity supplied rises (all else equal), giving an upward-sloping supply curve. Non-price determinants of supply include input costs, technology, and the number of producers.

---

## 3. Market equilibrium and price elasticity

**Equilibrium** occurs where quantity demanded equals quantity supplied. A price above equilibrium creates a **surplus** (pushing price down); a price below equilibrium creates a **shortage** (pushing price up).

**Price elasticity of demand (PED)** = Percentage change in quantity demanded ÷ Percentage change in price. Demand is **elastic** if |PED| > 1 (quantity responds proportionally more than price), **inelastic** if |PED| < 1 (quantity responds proportionally less), and **unitary** if |PED| = 1.

---

## 4. Market structures

- **Perfect competition** — many small firms, identical products, easy entry/exit, firms are price takers.
- **Monopoly** — a single firm, significant barriers to entry, firm is a price maker.
- **Oligopoly** — a few large, interdependent firms.
- **Monopolistic competition** — many firms with differentiated products, each having some limited pricing power.

---

## 5. National income

**Gross Domestic Product (GDP)** — the total market value of all final goods and services produced within a country's borders in a given period — is measured via three broadly equivalent approaches: the **output approach** (value added by sector), the **income approach** (sum of incomes earned — wages, rent, interest, profit), and the **expenditure approach** (consumption + investment + government spending + net exports).

---

## 6. Inflation

**Inflation** is a sustained increase in the general price level, reducing money's purchasing power, commonly measured via a **consumer price index (CPI)** tracking a representative basket of goods and services relative to a base period. Persistent high inflation erodes real incomes and savings, and creates uncertainty that can discourage investment; very low or negative inflation (deflation) can also be problematic, potentially signalling weak demand in the economy.

---

## 7. Unemployment

- **Frictional unemployment** — short-term, arising from people between jobs or newly entering the labour force.
- **Structural unemployment** — arising from a mismatch between workers' skills and the skills the economy currently demands.
- **Cyclical unemployment** — arising from a general downturn in overall economic activity.

---

## 8. Fiscal and monetary policy, basics

**Fiscal policy** — government use of spending and taxation to influence the economy (e.g. increasing government spending or cutting taxes to stimulate demand during a slowdown). **Monetary policy** — typically managed by a central bank, using tools such as interest rates and money supply management to influence borrowing, spending, investment, and inflation (e.g. raising interest rates to cool an overheating, high-inflation economy).

## 9. Full worked question — equilibrium and elasticity

**Scenario.** The price of a good rises from KSh 80 to KSh 100, and quantity demanded falls from 600 units to 540 units.

**Required:** (a) Compute the price elasticity of demand. (b) State, with a reason, whether demand is elastic or inelastic. (c) Explain the implication for a firm considering this price increase.

**Solution:**

(a) %ΔQd = (540 − 600) ÷ 600 × 100 = −10%. %ΔP = (100 − 80) ÷ 80 × 100 = +25%. PED = −10 ÷ 25 = **−0.4**.

(b) |PED| = 0.4 < 1, so demand is **inelastic** over this range.

(c) Since demand is inelastic, the price increase raises total revenue (quantity fell proportionally less than the price rose), suggesting the increase was likely a reasonable move from a revenue standpoint, though customer goodwill and competitor response should also be considered.

## 12. Exam technique and revision checklist

- Elasticity questions: always show the percentage-change workings for both price and quantity explicitly before dividing.
- Market-structure "distinguish" questions: use the same criteria (number of firms, barriers to entry, control over price) on both sides.
- National income questions: name all three approaches (output, income, expenditure) by name.
- Unemployment questions: pair each type with its specific cause, not a generic description.
- Fiscal vs monetary policy: fiscal = government (spending/tax); monetary = central bank (interest rates/money supply) — a very commonly confused distinction worth memorising precisely.

**Quick revision — one line per topic:**
1. Opportunity cost = value of the next best alternative foregone.
2. Law of demand: price ↑ → quantity demanded ↓. Law of supply: price ↑ → quantity supplied ↑.
3. PED = %Δ quantity demanded ÷ %Δ price; elastic if |PED|>1, inelastic if <1.
4. Market structures: perfect competition → monopolistic competition → oligopoly → monopoly.
5. GDP measured by output, income, or expenditure approach.
6. Inflation = sustained rise in general price level (measured via CPI).
7. Unemployment: frictional (job transition), structural (skills mismatch), cyclical (downturn).
8. Fiscal policy = government spending/tax; monetary policy = central bank interest rates/money supply.
