## 1. What is law, and the Kenyan legal system

**Law** is the body of rules, enforceable by the state, that governs conduct within a society. Kenya's legal system is organised around a court hierarchy — from subordinate courts, through the High Court, the Court of Appeal, up to the Supreme Court as the final appellate authority — with each level generally bound by the legal principles established by the courts above it.

---

## 2. Sources of law in Kenya

- **The Constitution** — the supreme law; any other law inconsistent with it is void to the extent of the inconsistency.
- **Legislation (statutes)** — Acts of Parliament and subsidiary legislation made under authority granted by an Act.
- **Case law (judicial precedent)** — principles established by courts in deciding cases, generally binding on lower courts in similar future cases.
- **Customary law** — customs of a community, recognised where not inconsistent with the Constitution or statute.
- **Common law and doctrines of equity** — inherited principles continuing to apply where not replaced by local statute.

---

## 3. The law of contract — essential elements

A **contract** is a legally binding agreement between two or more parties, requiring: **offer** (a clear proposal to be bound if accepted), **acceptance** (unqualified agreement to the exact terms offered), **consideration** (something of value given by each party), **intention to create legal relations**, **capacity** (legal ability to contract), and **legality of object** (the purpose must not be illegal or against public policy). A response introducing new terms is a **counter-offer**, which rejects the original offer rather than accepting it.

---

## 4. The law of contract — discharge and remedies

A contract is **discharged** by **performance** (both parties fully carry out their obligations), **agreement** (mutual variation/termination), **frustration** (an unforeseen event makes performance impossible through no fault of either party), or **breach** (failure to perform as agreed). Remedies for breach include **damages** (monetary compensation), **specific performance** (a court order compelling actual performance), **injunction** (a court order restraining an act), and **rescission** (cancelling the contract and restoring both parties to their pre-contract position).

---

## 5. Forms of business organisation

- **Sole proprietorship** — one owner, unlimited liability, simple to set up.
- **Partnership** — two or more owners sharing capital/management/profit under a partnership agreement, generally unlimited liability.
- **Private limited company** — a separate legal entity from its shareholders, giving owners limited liability, but more complex to set up and administer.
- **Public limited company** — able to offer shares to the public, subject to more extensive regulatory and disclosure requirements than a private company.

---

## 6. Introduction to company law

A registered **company** is a distinct legal person, separate from its shareholders and directors — it can own property, enter contracts, sue and be sued in its own name. This principle, known as the **separate legal personality** of a company, is what gives shareholders **limited liability** — their personal liability for the company's debts is limited to the amount they agreed to invest, protecting personal assets beyond that from the company's creditors (subject to limited exceptions where courts may "lift the corporate veil," e.g. in cases of fraud).

---

## 7. What is corporate governance?

**Corporate governance** is the system of rules, practices and processes by which a company is directed and controlled, balancing the interests of shareholders, management, and other stakeholders. Good governance typically requires: a properly constituted, functioning board of directors; clear accountability for decisions; transparent reporting; and effective internal controls — all aimed at protecting shareholders' and stakeholders' interests and building trust in the organisation.

---

## 8. Directors' duties, basics

Directors of a company owe **fiduciary duties** — duties of trust and good faith — to the company, commonly including: a duty to act within their powers and for a proper purpose, a duty to promote the success of the company, a duty to exercise independent judgement, a duty to exercise reasonable care, skill and diligence, and a duty to avoid conflicts of interest between their personal interests and the company's. Breach of these duties can expose a director to personal liability to the company.

## 9. Full worked question — contract validity analysis

**Scenario.** Wafula, aged 16, agrees to buy a laptop from a shop on credit, to be paid in three monthly instalments.

**Required:** (a) Identify the legal issue with this contract. (b) Explain the general rule and its practical implication.

**Solution:**

(a) The legal issue is **capacity** — Wafula is a minor and generally lacks full legal capacity to enter a binding contract.

(b) The general rule is that contracts with minors are generally not enforceable against the minor in the same way as contracts between adults (though certain limited exceptions exist, such as contracts for genuine "necessaries"). Practical implication: the shop may find it difficult to legally enforce the payment instalments against Wafula directly because of his age and limited contractual capacity, which is exactly why traders extending credit are expected to verify the age/capacity of the person they are contracting with before relying on the agreement.

## 12. Exam technique and revision checklist

- Court hierarchy questions: state the levels in the correct order, from lowest to highest.
- Contract questions: always work through the elements systematically before concluding whether a contract is valid — a scenario question is testing whether you can apply each element, not just recite them.
- "Distinguish" questions (business forms, private vs public company): use consistent criteria (liability, ownership, regulation) on both sides of the comparison.
- Separate legal personality and limited liability are related but distinct concepts — always explain both clearly if a question touches on either.
- Directors' duties questions: name each duty precisely (a specific fiduciary duty) rather than a vague description of "acting responsibly."

**Quick revision — one line per topic:**
1. Court hierarchy: subordinate courts → High Court → Court of Appeal → Supreme Court.
2. Sources of law: Constitution, legislation, case law, customary law, common law/equity.
3. Contract needs: offer, acceptance, consideration, intention, capacity, legality of object.
4. Contracts discharged by: performance, agreement, frustration, breach.
5. Business forms: sole proprietorship (unlimited liability) → partnership (shared, unlimited) → private company (limited liability, separate entity) → public company (can offer shares publicly).
6. Separate legal personality: company is distinct from its owners; gives rise to limited liability.
7. Corporate governance: rules/practices/processes directing and controlling a company, balancing stakeholder interests.
8. Directors' fiduciary duties: proper purpose, promote success, independent judgement, care/skill/diligence, avoid conflicts of interest.
