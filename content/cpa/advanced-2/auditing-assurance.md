## 1. What auditing is (and is not)

**Audit (working definition).** An **independent examination** of financial information of an entity, with a view to expressing an **opinion** on whether that information is **presented fairly, in all material respects**, in accordance with an identified financial reporting framework.

An audit is *not*:
- A guarantee that the financial statements are 100% correct.
- Insurance against fraud (though the auditor considers fraud risk).
- A management-consulting service (though management may benefit from findings).
- A substitute for management's own responsibility for the accounts.

The output of an audit is an **audit report** conveying the auditor's **opinion** — nothing more, nothing less.

---

## 2. Auditing vs accounting

| Dimension | Accounting | Auditing |
|---|---|---|
| Who performs it | Company staff / accountants | Independent external auditor |
| Purpose | Prepare and present financial information | Give an opinion on financial information already prepared |
| Timing | Continuous during the year | After the accounts are drawn up |
| Governing framework | Financial reporting framework (e.g. IFRS) | Auditing standards (ISAs) + ethical code |
| Output | Financial statements | Audit report and opinion |
| Ends with | Trial balance → financial statements | Audit opinion (and management letter) |

Auditing **starts where accounting ends**.

## 3. Objectives of an audit and "true and fair"

**Primary objective (per ISAs):** to enable the auditor to express an opinion on whether the financial statements are prepared, in all material respects, in accordance with the applicable financial reporting framework and give a **true and fair view** (or "present fairly").

**"True and fair"** is not statutorily defined but is understood professionally to mean:
- Free from **material misstatement**.
- Prepared using **appropriate and consistently applied** accounting policies.
- Disclosing all matters required by the framework and by law.
- Reflecting the **substance** of transactions, not merely their legal form.
- Reasonable in the light of the entity's circumstances.

**Secondary (by-product) objectives:**
- Detection and deterrence of **error** and **fraud** (auditor plans to obtain reasonable, not absolute, assurance).
- Provision of a **management letter** identifying internal control weaknesses.
- Advice on statutory compliance (companies legislation, tax, sector regulation).

## 4. Why an audit exists — the agency problem

Modern limited-liability companies separate **ownership** (shareholders) from **management** (directors). Shareholders delegate day-to-day control to directors under an implicit agency relationship. This creates two frictions:

**(a) Information asymmetry** — directors know the true position; shareholders see only what directors report.

**(b) Goal conflict** — directors can be tempted to prioritise their own remuneration, security, or empire-building over shareholder value.

**Mechanisms that mitigate the agency problem:**
- **Aligning incentives** — profit-linked bonuses, share options, performance-based pay.
- **Monitoring** — non-executive directors, audit committees, and *external audit*.
- **Statutory safeguards** — the Companies Act mandates proper books, published accounts, an AGM, and the appointment of an independent auditor to lend credibility to the accounts the directors themselves prepare.

The audit therefore exists to give shareholders (and other stakeholders) confidence that the directors' own account of their stewardship is reliable.

## 5. Users of audited financial statements

- **Shareholders and potential investors** — investment decisions, dividend expectations.
- **Lenders and prospective lenders** — credit decisions, covenant compliance.
- **Suppliers and trade creditors** — credit-term decisions.
- **Customers** — assessing the entity's continuity, especially for long-term contracts.
- **Employees and trade unions** — pay negotiations, job security.
- **Government and regulators** — tax assessment, sector regulation.
- **The public** — corporate social responsibility, community impact.
- **Analysts and rating agencies** — informing their ratings and reports.

## 6. Types of audit

**By statutory basis:**
- **Statutory audit** — required by law (e.g. Companies Act for public/large private companies).
- **Private (non-statutory) audit** — voluntary; scope agreed between auditor and client.

**By coverage:**
- **Financial statement audit** — opinion on the annual accounts.
- **Interim audit** — work done during the year to ease year-end pressure.
- **Continuous audit** — ongoing checks throughout the year (banks, brokers).
- **Final audit** — carried out only after year-end.

**By purpose or objective:**
- **Internal audit** — appraisal function established by management; part of the entity.
- **External audit** — independent, statutory, expresses opinion for third parties.
- **Operational / management audit** — evaluates efficiency and effectiveness of operations.
- **Value-for-money (VFM) audit** — public sector; economy, efficiency, effectiveness.
- **Forensic audit** — investigation into suspected fraud, litigation support.
- **Compliance audit** — adherence to laws, regulations, contracts.
- **Environmental / social audit** — sustainability and ESG reporting.
- **Systems / IT audit** — controls over computerised information systems.

**Internal vs external audit — similarities and differences:**

Similarities: both use similar techniques (walkthroughs, sampling, analytical review); both require independence in mind and appearance; both produce written reports.

Differences: internal auditors report to management/audit committee; external auditors report to shareholders. Internal auditors focus on *risk management, control, and governance processes*; external auditors focus on the *fair presentation* of the financial statements.

## 7. Stages of an audit engagement

1. **Client acceptance / continuance** — background check, independence assessment, ethical clearance.
2. **Engagement letter** — set out scope, responsibilities, deliverables, fees (ISA 210).
3. **Planning (ISA 300)** — understand the entity, identify risks, set materiality, design the audit strategy.
4. **Understanding the entity and internal control (ISA 315)** — including CIS environment.
5. **Risk assessment and response (ISA 330)** — plan the mix of tests of control and substantive procedures.
6. **Tests of control** — evaluate whether controls operated effectively during the period.
7. **Substantive procedures** — analytical procedures and tests of detail on transactions and balances.
8. **Completion and review** — subsequent events, going concern, management representations, overall review.
9. **Reporting (ISA 700 series)** — issue the auditor's report; deliver management letter.

## 8. The auditor and the Companies Act

The Companies Act (or successor statute) sets the legal framework in which the external auditor operates: **appointment, qualifications, remuneration, tenure, removal, rights, and duties**.

**Who may be appointed:** a member in good standing of the professional accountancy institute recognised by the Act (in Kenya, ICPAK), holding a valid practising certificate.

**Who may *not* be appointed:**
- An officer or servant of the company.
- A partner or employee of an officer/servant of the company.
- A body corporate.
- A person who is disqualified from acting as auditor of any subsidiary or holding company of the entity.

The rules exist to preserve **independence in fact and in appearance**.

## 9. Auditor appointment, removal, resignation, and remuneration

**Appointment**
- **First auditors** — appointed by the directors before the first AGM; hold office until the conclusion of that AGM.
- **Subsequent auditors** — appointed by the shareholders at each AGM; hold office until the next AGM.
- **Casual vacancy** — the directors may fill it; the appointee holds office until the next AGM.
- **Failure of members to appoint** — the Registrar/relevant authority may appoint on application.

**Remuneration** — fixed by whoever appoints the auditor (directors for first auditors and casual vacancies; shareholders in general meeting otherwise), or as delegated by ordinary resolution.

**Removal** — a company may, by ordinary resolution at a general meeting, remove an auditor before expiry of the term, provided special notice has been given. The auditor being removed has the right to:
- Receive the notice.
- Make written representations to members.
- Speak at the meeting at which the removal is proposed.

**Resignation** — the auditor may resign by written notice, which must contain either a statement that there are no circumstances that ought to be brought to the attention of members and creditors, **or** a statement of those circumstances. This is the "**statement of circumstances**" that protects stakeholders and future auditors.

## 10. Rights and duties of the statutory auditor

**Rights (to enable the auditor to perform the job):**
- Right of access **at all times** to the books, accounts, and vouchers of the company.
- Right to require information and explanations from officers of the company.
- Right to receive notices of, and attend, general meetings.
- Right to be heard on any part of the business that concerns the auditor.
- Right to remuneration as agreed.

**Duties:**
- Report to members on the accounts examined and every balance sheet and profit and loss account laid before the company in general meeting.
- State expressly whether, in the auditor's opinion, the accounts give a true and fair view.
- State whether proper books of account have been kept.
- State whether the accounts agree with the books and returns.
- Report on whether information and explanations sought were received.
- Report on any specific matters required by the Act (e.g. directors' loans, related party disclosures).

## 11. Auditor independence, ethics, and negligence

**The five fundamental ethical principles (IESBA Code):**
- **Integrity** — straightforward and honest.
- **Objectivity** — no bias, conflict of interest, or undue influence.
- **Professional competence and due care** — maintain skills; act diligently.
- **Confidentiality** — respect information obtained; use only for legitimate purposes.
- **Professional behaviour** — comply with laws and avoid disrepute.

**Threats to independence:**
- **Self-interest** — fee dependence, personal financial interests.
- **Self-review** — auditing one's own work (e.g. accounting services provided to audit clients).
- **Advocacy** — promoting the client's position (e.g. representing the client in litigation).
- **Familiarity** — long association, close personal relationships.
- **Intimidation** — threats, aggressive behaviour by the client.

**Safeguards** — rotation of the engagement partner, separation of teams for non-audit services, independent quality reviews, ethics-committee oversight, refusing/relinquishing engagements that cannot be safeguarded.

**Confidentiality — exceptions where disclosure is required or permitted:**
- Where compelled by legal process.
- Where required by statute (e.g. anti-money-laundering reporting).
- To defend oneself in disciplinary or legal proceedings.
- With the client's express permission.

**Auditor's liability — negligence to third parties.** For a third party to succeed in a negligence claim, the classic elements must be shown:
- The auditor **owed a duty of care** to the claimant.
- The auditor **breached** that duty (fell below the standard of a reasonably competent professional).
- The claimant **suffered a loss**.
- The loss was **caused** by the breach (foreseeable and not too remote).

Kenyan and common-law authorities (in the tradition of *Caparo Industries plc v Dickman*) generally restrict auditor liability to persons and purposes reasonably in contemplation at the time of the audit.

## 12. Corporate governance and the audit committee

**Corporate governance** — the system by which companies are directed and controlled. The board of directors should:
- Set the strategic direction and risk appetite.
- Provide leadership within a framework of prudent and effective controls.
- Set values and standards, ensuring obligations to shareholders and other stakeholders are met.
- Establish appropriate board committees (audit, remuneration, nomination).

**Role of the audit committee:**
- Recommend the appointment and remuneration of the external auditor.
- Review the scope and results of the external audit and internal audit.
- Oversee financial reporting integrity.
- Oversee the internal control and risk management systems.
- Provide a channel of communication independent of executive management.

**Benefits:**
- Improved communication between directors, external auditor, and management.
- Strengthened auditor independence.
- Greater confidence of the investing public.
- Better oversight of internal audit function.

## 13. The engagement letter (ISA 210)

The engagement letter formalises the auditor–client relationship **before** work begins.

**Purpose:**
- Confirm the auditor's acceptance of the appointment.
- Set out the **scope** and **objective** of the audit.
- Clarify the **responsibilities** of management (preparation of financial statements, internal control, providing access) and of the auditor (opinion under ISAs).
- Confirm the applicable financial reporting framework.
- Confirm the form and content of the expected report.
- Reduce the risk of **misunderstanding**.
- Document the basis on which fees will be charged.
- Confirm in writing any verbal arrangements reached during acceptance.

An engagement letter should be issued for **each engagement** and reconsidered whenever there is a change in circumstances (change of management, ownership, scope, or reporting framework, or a significant lapse of time since the previous letter).

## 14. Risk and materiality (ISA 315, ISA 320)

**Audit risk (AR) = Inherent risk (IR) × Control risk (CR) × Detection risk (DR).**

- **Inherent risk** — susceptibility of an assertion to material misstatement, before considering controls.
- **Control risk** — the risk that a misstatement will not be prevented or detected on a timely basis by internal control.
- **Detection risk** — the risk that the auditor's procedures will not detect a material misstatement.

The auditor **cannot control** inherent or control risk (these are properties of the entity), but can **manage detection risk** by adjusting the nature, timing, and extent of procedures.

**Materiality** — information is material if its omission or misstatement could influence the economic decisions of users taken on the basis of the financial statements.

**Two levels:**
- **Overall (planning) materiality** — usually a percentage benchmark (e.g. 5% of profit before tax, 1% of revenue, 1–2% of total assets — the choice depends on the entity).
- **Performance materiality** — a lower threshold used for individual account balances/transactions to reduce the aggregate risk of undetected misstatements exceeding overall materiality.

Materiality is reassessed throughout the audit as new information emerges.

## 15. Financial-statement assertions

Assertions are the implicit or explicit claims made by management in the financial statements. Auditors design procedures to test each assertion.

**Assertions about classes of transactions and events:**
- **Occurrence** — recorded transactions actually took place and pertain to the entity.
- **Completeness** — all transactions that should have been recorded have been.
- **Accuracy** — amounts and data are recorded appropriately.
- **Cut-off** — transactions are recorded in the correct accounting period.
- **Classification** — transactions are recorded in the proper accounts.

**Assertions about account balances at year-end:**
- **Existence** — assets, liabilities, and equity actually exist.
- **Rights and obligations** — the entity holds or controls the rights to assets, and liabilities are obligations of the entity.
- **Completeness** — all balances that should have been recorded are recorded.
- **Valuation and allocation** — balances are included in the financial statements at appropriate amounts.

**Assertions about presentation and disclosure:**
- **Occurrence and rights and obligations.**
- **Completeness.**
- **Classification and understandability.**
- **Accuracy and valuation.**

## 16. Chapter recap and self-assessment questions

**Recap:** The audit exists to lend credibility to management-prepared financial statements. It is an independent examination performed under ISAs, delivering an opinion — not a guarantee. The auditor is appointed under statute, owes rights and duties to the members, must be independent in fact and appearance, and works to manage audit risk through informed materiality judgements and assertion-focused procedures.

**Q1.** Distinguish auditing from accounting under any four dimensions. *(4 marks)*

*Model answer:* See the table in Section 2 — who performs it, purpose, timing, output. Any 4.

**Q2.** Explain the "true and fair view" concept. *(3 marks)*

*Model answer:* Free from material misstatement; prepared using appropriate and consistently applied policies; disclosing everything the framework and law require; reflecting substance over form; reasonable in the light of the entity's circumstances.

**Q3.** State the agency problem and identify three mechanisms that mitigate it. *(4 marks)*

*Model answer:* Owners delegate to directors, creating information asymmetry and goal conflict. Mitigation: incentive alignment (performance pay/share options); monitoring (non-executives, audit committee, external audit); statutory safeguards (Companies Act — proper books, published accounts, AGM, independent auditor).

**Q4.** State the three components of audit risk and identify which of them the auditor can *directly* control. *(3 marks)*

*Model answer:* Inherent risk, control risk, detection risk. Only detection risk is directly controllable by the auditor (through nature, timing, and extent of procedures).

**Q5.** Distinguish overall (planning) materiality from performance materiality. *(3 marks)*

*Model answer:* Overall materiality is the threshold for the financial statements as a whole; performance materiality is a lower amount set for individual balances/classes so that the aggregate of undetected and known misstatements does not exceed overall materiality.

**Q6.** List any five contents of an engagement letter. *(5 marks)*

*Model answer:* Confirmation of acceptance; objective and scope; management responsibilities; auditor responsibilities under ISAs; applicable reporting framework; expected form of report; fee basis; access requirements; unrestricted communication with predecessor auditor; acknowledgment signature.

**Q7.** State any five persons who may **not** be appointed auditor of a company. *(5 marks)*

*Model answer:* Officer or servant of the company; partner or employee of such officer/servant; a body corporate; a person disqualified in respect of a related company; a person who is not a member in good standing of the recognised professional body / lacks a practising certificate.

**Q8.** Identify four threats to auditor independence and give one safeguard for each. *(8 marks)*

*Model answer:* Self-interest (rotate engagement partner, limit fee dependence); self-review (separate teams for non-audit services); advocacy (decline advocacy engagements); familiarity (mandatory partner rotation, cooling-off periods); intimidation (escalation protocols and, ultimately, resignation).

**Q9.** State any five assertions the auditor tests on year-end account balances. *(5 marks)*

*Model answer:* Existence; rights and obligations; completeness; valuation and allocation; (plus, for related presentation, classification and understandability).

**Q10.** List three benefits of an effective audit committee. *(3 marks)*

*Model answer:* Improved communication between board, management, and auditors; strengthened auditor independence; better oversight of internal audit and financial reporting; enhanced investor confidence. *(Any 3.)*

## 17. Exam technique and revision checklist

- Every question about "the objective of an audit" should mention **opinion**, **material**, **true and fair**, and the applicable **framework** — omitting any of these loses marks.
- On risk/materiality questions, use the **AR = IR × CR × DR** formula explicitly, and identify **detection risk** as the only auditor-controllable component.
- On the auditor–client relationship, **quote ISA numbers** where confident (ISA 210, 300, 315, 320, 330, 500, 700) — even one correct citation strengthens the answer.
- On ethics questions, **name the threat** (self-interest, self-review, advocacy, familiarity, intimidation) rather than describing it generically.
- On statutory questions (appointment/removal/rights/duties), quote the **procedural safeguards** (special notice, right to representations, statement of circumstances on resignation) — these are the mark-scoring specifics.
- On assertions, group them by transactions / balances / presentation so the examiner sees you understand the ISA 315 framework rather than reciting a random list.

**One-line recap:**
1. Audit = independent examination → opinion on true and fair view.
2. Exists because of the shareholder–director agency problem.
3. Governed by ISAs; ethics anchored by the IESBA Code.
4. Companies Act sets appointment, tenure, removal, rights, and duties.
5. Independence is judged in fact **and** appearance; five recurring threats.
6. Audit risk = IR × CR × DR — plan procedures to manage DR.
7. Materiality is quantitative + qualitative; overall vs performance.
8. Assertions drive procedures — test each explicitly.
9. Audit committee bridges board, management, and auditors.
10. Report is the deliverable — everything else is preparation for it.
