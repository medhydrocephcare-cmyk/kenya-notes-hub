## 1. Nature and scope of public finance

**Public finance** is the branch of economics concerned with **how governments raise revenue**, **how they spend it**, **how they borrow to bridge the gap**, and **how those activities are administered**.

The scope is conventionally divided into four categories:

- **Public revenue** — income of the state from taxes, fees, fines, penalties, income from state property, loan repayments, sale of state assets, royalties, and grants.
- **Public expenditure** — how the state spends its money on services, transfers, and investments; includes the principles ("canons") governing spending and its effects on production, employment, distribution, and stability.
- **Public debt** — borrowing to close the gap between revenue and expenditure; includes reasons for borrowing, methods, sources, debt burden, and redemption.
- **Financial administration** — the systems for collecting, custodying, and disbursing public money, coordinating expenditure to plan, managing debt, and auditing the state's financial operations. It includes budget preparation, execution, and audit.

**Where does public finance fit in economics?** Under certain conditions private markets allocate resources efficiently, but in the presence of **market failures** (public goods, externalities, monopoly, information asymmetries, distributional concerns) the government has a legitimate role. Public finance is the analytical toolkit for evaluating that role.

## 2. Public finance vs private finance

**Similarities:**
- Both aim at the satisfaction of human wants (social wants for the state, individual wants for private actors).
- Both must **balance income against expenditure** over the medium term.
- Both use rational decision-making tools (budgets, cost–benefit analysis, opportunity cost).
- Both are concerned with **stewardship** and accountability.

**Differences:**

| Dimension | Public finance | Private finance |
|---|---|---|
| Objective | Maximise social welfare | Maximise individual/private utility or profit |
| Income determination | Expenditure is planned first, then revenue is raised to match | Income is largely given; expenditure is adjusted to fit |
| Coercive power | Can tax and enforce compliance | Depends on voluntary market transactions |
| Time horizon | Long-term; intergenerational | Typically shorter, individual life-cycle |
| Currency issuance | The state can issue legal tender | Individuals cannot |
| Publicity | Public accounts, published budgets, parliamentary scrutiny | Largely private; subject only to statutory disclosure |
| Elasticity of income | Highly elastic (tax rates, base changes) | Relatively inelastic in the short run |

**Key insight:** private finance adjusts *outlays* to income; public finance adjusts *income* to planned outlays.

## 3. Functions of the public sector

Musgrave's classical framework identifies **three** economic functions of the public sector:

**(a) Allocation function** — providing public goods and correcting for externalities. The state provides goods that the market undersupplies (defence, public roads, basic research, public health) and taxes negative externalities (pollution, congestion).

**(b) Distribution function** — redistributing income and wealth. Instruments include progressive taxation, targeted transfers (cash transfers, food subsidies), public education and health, and estate/inheritance taxes.

**(c) Stabilisation function** — using fiscal policy to stabilise the macro-economy (managing aggregate demand, controlling inflation, smoothing the business cycle, managing external balances). Coordinated with monetary policy conducted by the central bank.

A fourth, often added, is:

**(d) Development / growth function** — investing in infrastructure, human capital, and institutions to expand the economy's productive capacity.

## 4. External resource financing

**External resources** are financial inflows into the government from **outside** the country — loans, grants, and technical assistance from foreign governments, multilateral institutions, and other partners.

**Institutional setting in Kenya:** the **External Resources Department (ERD)** in the National Treasury is the focal point for negotiating, receiving, and managing external funds.

**Roles of the ERD:**
- Coordinate the mobilisation of external finance.
- Negotiate loan and grant agreements on behalf of the government.
- Book-keep and manage donor funds in accordance with each agreement.
- Monitor implementation of donor-financed projects, often through client-connection systems and platforms like e-ProMIS.
- Liaise with development partners and enforce contractual obligations.

**Role of ambassadors, high commissioners, and other diplomats:**
- Identify and initiate external funding opportunities in their host countries.
- Introduce Kenyan proposals to development partners.
- Facilitate negotiations and follow up on disbursement.
- Represent Kenya at donor coordination fora.

**Categories of external resources:**
- **Concessional loans** — long tenor, low interest, from multilaterals (IMF, World Bank, AfDB) and bilateral donors.
- **Commercial external borrowing** — sovereign eurobonds and syndicated loans.
- **Grants** — non-repayable, often tied to a specific programme or project.
- **Technical assistance** — capacity building, advisers, equipment.

**Risks of over-reliance on external financing:** exchange-rate risk, refinancing risk, conditionality, loss of policy autonomy, and vulnerability to donor cycles.

## 5. Public investments and state corporations

**Public investments** are government commitments of resources — through wholly owned entities, joint ventures, and equity stakes — into productive activities.

**Institutional players:**

- **Government Investment Departments** — mandated to plan and monitor state investments across ministries.
- **State Corporations** — legal persons established under the State Corporations Act (Cap 446), tasked with commercial, service delivery, regulatory, or advisory roles.
- **Public Investments Board / KenInvest** — promotes and facilitates investment in Kenya, coordinating incentives and information for domestic and foreign investors.
- **Public Investments Committee (PIC)** — a parliamentary committee that scrutinises the accounts and management of state corporations and reports to Parliament.
- **Auditor-General** — audits state corporations and reports findings that inform PIC deliberations.
- **National Treasury** — sets overall investment policy and approves major transactions.

**Governance of a state corporation** typically vests in a **Board of Directors** appointed under the founding Act, with clearly defined ministerial responsibility, a chief executive, and committees (audit, strategy & investment promotion, human resources).

**Purposes of state corporations:**
- Deliver essential services where private supply is inadequate.
- Regulate strategic sectors.
- Achieve social objectives (equitable distribution, employment).
- Retain public control over strategic assets.

**Common weaknesses:** political interference, soft budget constraints, weak governance, and inefficiency. Reform tools include commercialisation, corporatisation, privatisation, and PPPs.

## 6. Public–Private Partnerships (PPPs)

A **PPP** is a long-term contract between a public authority (the "contracting authority") and a private party for the delivery of public infrastructure or services, with **shared risk, financing, and rewards**.

**Legal framework:** the PPP Act, 2013 establishes:

- The **PPP Unit** within the National Treasury — the secretariat and technical arm of the PPP Committee.
- The **PPP Committee** (or successor body) — approves PPP projects at key milestones.
- The **PPP Petition Committee** — hears complaints from bidders about procurement decisions.
- The **PPP Project Facilitation Fund** — provides seed capital for feasibility studies, government contributions, and viability-gap funding.

**Common PPP structures:**
- Build–Operate–Transfer (BOT).
- Build–Own–Operate (BOO).
- Design–Build–Finance–Operate (DBFO).
- Concession contracts.
- Management contracts.
- Lease–develop–operate.

**Project cycle:**
1. Identification and screening (contracting authority; sector diagnostic study).
2. Feasibility study and value-for-money assessment.
3. Approval by the PPP Committee.
4. Procurement (competitive bidding).
5. Negotiation and execution of the project agreement.
6. Financial close.
7. Construction and operation.
8. Handover / termination.

**Role of the PPP Unit (illustrative):**
- Serve as secretariat and technical arm of the Committee.
- Provide technical, financial, and legal expertise.
- Support contracting authorities across the project cycle.
- Manage the PPP database and information system.

**Benefits:** access to private capital and expertise; risk transfer to the party best able to manage each risk; whole-life costing; potential efficiency gains.

**Risks:** contingent liabilities on the government; complex contracts; renegotiation risk; public opposition; long lock-in.

## 7. Public debt management

**Public debt** is the aggregate liability the state owes to domestic residents and non-residents, arising from borrowing to finance the gap between public revenue and expenditure.

**Debt Management Office (DMO)** — in Kenya, the Public Debt Management Office in the National Treasury is responsible for:
- Executing the government's borrowing programme.
- Servicing debt as it falls due.
- Maintaining the debt register and reporting to Parliament and the public.
- Advising the Cabinet Secretary on debt strategy.

---

## 8. Debt classification and sources

**By source:**
- **Internal (domestic) debt** — Treasury bills, Treasury bonds, tap sales, infrastructure bonds — subscribed by domestic residents and institutions.
- **External debt** — concessional and commercial borrowing from foreign governments, multilaterals, and international capital markets.

**By tenor:**
- **Short-term** — up to 1 year (Treasury bills).
- **Medium-term** — 1–5 years.
- **Long-term** — over 5 years (long-dated bonds, eurobonds).

**By collateral/security:**
- **Funded debt** — with a defined redemption fund set aside.
- **Unfunded debt** — no dedicated sinking fund.

**By redeemability:**
- **Redeemable** — has a fixed maturity date.
- **Irredeemable / perpetual** — no fixed maturity (rare for sovereign debt).

**By whom:**
- **Central government debt.**
- **County government debt** — narrowly circumscribed under PFM Act; requires National Treasury guarantees and Parliament approval.
- **Guaranteed debt** — obligations of state corporations backed by government guarantee.

## 9. Debt management strategy and the budget cycle

**Objectives of public debt management:**
1. Meet the government's financing needs at the lowest possible cost, subject to a prudent degree of risk.
2. Ensure the sustainability of the debt burden.
3. Share the costs and benefits of borrowing fairly between current and future generations.
4. Develop and deepen the domestic debt market.

**Medium-Term Debt Management Strategy (MTDS):** published annually, sets out target composition of new borrowing (domestic vs external, fixed vs variable, currency mix, maturity profile) and expected debt trajectory.

**Relationship with the budget process:**
- The budget determines the fiscal deficit — the primary driver of the borrowing requirement.
- The MTDS translates the deficit into a borrowing plan by instrument and tenor.
- The Treasury Single Account and quarterly cash plans align actual borrowing with cash needs.
- Reports on debt stock, servicing, and new issuance feed back into the next budget cycle.

**Comparative EAC context:** East African peers face similar issues — rising commercial external borrowing, growing domestic debt, and pressure on debt-service to revenue ratios. Coordinated approaches through the EAC monetary integration workstream address convergence criteria on debt sustainability.

## 10. Debt management by county governments

Under the PFM Act, county governments may borrow only:
- With the guarantee of the National Government.
- With the approval of the County Assembly.
- Where the loan is used to finance development expenditure.
- Where the loan meets the terms and conditions set by the National Treasury.

**Records the County Treasury must maintain for every loan:**
- Purpose and terms of the loan.
- Amount borrowed and disbursement schedule.
- Repayment schedule (principal, interest, other charges).
- Outstanding balance at any time.
- Guarantees and securities pledged.
- Any conditions or covenants attached.

Reporting flows upward to the National Treasury and Parliament for oversight.

## 11. Institutional players in Kenyan public finance

- **National Treasury** — coordinates public financial management; prepares the national budget; issues debt.
- **Cabinet Secretary, National Treasury** — political head; has statutory powers on debt, guarantees, and grants.
- **Parliament (National Assembly and Senate)** — appropriates funds, approves borrowing limits, oversees expenditure.
- **Controller of Budget** — authorises withdrawals from public funds; reports on budget implementation.
- **Auditor-General** — audits public entities; reports to Parliament.
- **Central Bank of Kenya** — fiscal agent of the government; conducts monetary policy; issues government securities.
- **Public Investments Committee (PIC), Public Accounts Committee (PAC)** — parliamentary oversight of state corporations and government accounts respectively.
- **Kenya Revenue Authority (KRA)** — primary tax collector.
- **County treasuries and County Assemblies** — mirror at devolved level.
- **Independent commissions** (e.g. Commission on Revenue Allocation) — advise on equitable sharing.

## 12. Worked scenario — a PPP evaluation

**Scenario (original).** Kilimanjaro County wishes to build a 40-km inter-town water pipeline. Two options are on the table:

- **Option A — traditional public procurement.** County borrows KSh 3.2 billion at 12% over 20 years; contractor builds; county operates.
- **Option B — DBFO PPP.** A consortium designs, builds, finances, and operates the pipeline for 25 years, charging the county an annual availability fee of KSh 380 million; the asset reverts to the county at the end of the concession.

**Required:** Outline the key criteria the county should apply in choosing between the two options.

**Model answer**

1. **Value for money (VFM).** Compare the present value of lifetime costs under each option using an appropriate discount rate. The PPP typically has higher headline cost of capital but may deliver savings through construction-cost certainty, whole-life maintenance discipline, and better risk allocation.

2. **Risk transfer.** Under Option A the county carries construction risk, cost overrun, and long-term maintenance risk. Under Option B most of these risks pass to the private partner (payment is conditional on availability). Correct pricing of the risk premium is central.

3. **Affordability.** Availability payments are a firm multi-year commitment. The county must assess its future revenue base and legal borrowing headroom, and account for the PPP as a contingent liability where the standards require.

4. **Fiscal accounting treatment.** Whether the pipeline sits on the county's balance sheet under IPSAS 32 or is off-balance-sheet determines the debt disclosure impact.

5. **Legal and institutional readiness.** Compliance with the PPP Act, PPP Committee approval, procurement rules, and Attorney-General clearance.

6. **Environmental and social safeguards.** Both options require ESIA; PPPs demand contractual monitoring provisions.

7. **Sustainability of user tariffs and cross-subsidies.** How the annual availability payment is funded (tariffs, block grant, subvention).

8. **Contract governance.** The PPP option needs a robust contract-management team over 25 years; failure to invest in this capability erodes VFM.

**Recommendation format:** state the recommended option, tied to the highest-weighted criterion (usually VFM adjusted for risk).

## 14. Exam technique and revision checklist

- On "nature and scope" questions, always split the answer into the **four scope categories** — examiners award marks for the completeness of the frame.
- On functions of government, quote **Musgrave** by name and cover **allocation, distribution, stabilisation** (add development if the marks are high).
- On PPPs, walk through the **project cycle in order**, and pair each benefit with its **matching risk** (private capital vs contingent liability, risk transfer vs contract complexity, etc.).
- On debt management, cite **objectives before instruments** and mention the **MTDS** by acronym at least once.
- On external financing, remember to include the **diplomatic role** (ambassadors, high commissioners) — many candidates forget this and lose easy marks.
- Where the syllabus references a specific Act (PPP Act 2013, PFM Act, State Corporations Act Cap 446), name it — a numbered citation adds credibility even if you cannot remember the exact section.

**One-line revision recap:**
1. Public finance = revenue + expenditure + debt + administration.
2. Public finance plans outlays first, then finds revenue; private finance is the reverse.
3. Three classical functions: allocation, distribution, stabilisation.
4. ERD in the National Treasury coordinates external resource financing.
5. State corporations are legal persons under Cap 446, overseen by Parliament (PIC).
6. PPPs governed by the PPP Act 2013 — Unit, Committee, Petition Committee, Facilitation Fund.
7. Debt management aims at least-cost financing consistent with sustainability.
8. MTDS translates budget deficit into a borrowing plan.
9. Counties borrow only with a national guarantee and County Assembly approval.
10. PPP choice = VFM + risk transfer + affordability + legal compliance + contract governance.
